# Can You Trademark an AI-Generated Logo? The Legal Landscape in 2026

[IMAGE 1 PLACEHOLDER — Persona Scenario]

The question comes up in nearly every Lovart demo: "If I generate a logo with AI, can I actually own it? Can I trademark it? What is stopping someone else from generating the same logo and using it?"

These are not hypothetical questions. In 2026, AI-generated logos are moving from experimental side projects to the primary visual identities of venture-backed startups, DTC brands, and local businesses. The legal framework governing these logos is still taking shape — court rulings are accumulating, policy guidance is evolving, and the answers depend on nuanced distinctions that most non-lawyers have never encountered.

This article maps the current legal landscape as of October 2026, drawing on USPTO guidance, Copyright Office rulings, and key court decisions. We focus on U.S. law, with notes on EU and UK divergence where relevant. And we explain exactly how Lovart's architecture navigates these complexities to give you the strongest possible claim to ownership.

## The Fundamental Distinction: Copyright vs. Trademark

[IMAGE 2 PLACEHOLDER — Conceptual Diagram]

The first thing to understand is that copyright and trademark are entirely different legal regimes with different requirements, different protections, and different implications for AI-generated work. Most confusion about AI logos comes from conflating the two.

**Copyright** protects original creative works — the artistic expression embodied in a logo design. It attaches automatically when a work is created and gives the owner exclusive rights to reproduce, distribute, and create derivative works. Copyright requires human authorship — a principle that has become the central battleground in AI copyright law.

**Trademark** protects brand identifiers — words, symbols, designs, or combinations thereof that distinguish the source of goods or services in commerce. Trademark is not about creative expression; it is about consumer protection and marketplace clarity. You do not trademark a logo because it is beautiful; you trademark it because it tells consumers "this product comes from this company."

This distinction is critical because it means the hurdles for AI-generated content are different in each regime. Copyright has been the bigger problem for AI outputs. Trademark, by contrast, cares less about how a logo was created and more about whether it actually functions as a source identifier in the marketplace.

## The Copyright Question: Who Is the Author?

The U.S. Copyright Office has been emphatically clear on one point: works created entirely by AI, without meaningful human creative input, are not eligible for copyright protection. This position was formalized in the Copyright Office's March 2025 policy guidance and has been upheld in multiple cases, most notably *Thaler v. Perlmutter*, where the D.C. Circuit affirmed that "human authorship is a bedrock requirement of copyright."

But "entirely by AI" is doing a lot of work in that sentence. The Copyright Office has also recognized that AI-assisted works — where a human makes creative choices that shape the final output — can be protected. The question is: where is the line?

The courts and the Copyright Office have coalesced around a framework that looks at three factors:

**1. The specificity of human creative control.** Did the human provide detailed creative direction, or was the AI operating autonomously? A prompt like "a cool logo for a coffee shop" is likely insufficient. A prompt like "a minimalist geometric logo featuring a coffee bean integrated into a mountain silhouette, using a warm brown and cream palette with negative-space letterform 'A' embedded in the mountain peak" involves far more creative decision-making.

**2. The degree of human selection and refinement.** Did the human curate from multiple AI outputs, select one, and then modify it? The Copyright Office has indicated that selecting among AI-generated options and applying modifications can constitute sufficient human authorship — particularly when the modifications are themselves creative.

**3. The nature of the AI tool.** Is the AI operating as a tool under human creative direction (like a camera or a paintbrush), or is it operating as an autonomous creator (like a commissioned artist)? This distinction — tool versus author — is where Lovart's architecture becomes relevant.

## The Trademark Question: Can You File?

[IMAGE 3 PLACEHOLDER — Real UI Screenshot]

The good news: trademark law is more accommodating of AI-generated logos than copyright law, because trademark cares about use in commerce, not creative authorship.

**You can file a trademark application for an AI-generated logo.** The USPTO does not ask how your logo was created. It asks whether your logo is distinctive, whether it is being used in commerce, and whether it is likely to cause confusion with existing marks. The creation method is irrelevant to these inquiries.

The requirements for trademark registration are:
1. **Distinctiveness:** The logo must be capable of identifying and distinguishing your goods or services from others. A generic or merely descriptive logo will be refused regardless of how it was created.
2. **Use in commerce:** You must be using the logo in the actual sale or advertising of goods or services in interstate commerce (or have a bona fide intent to do so).
3. **No likelihood of confusion:** Your logo must not be confusingly similar to an existing registered or common-law mark for related goods or services.

None of these criteria depend on whether a human or an AI drew the logo. A distinctive, commercially-used, non-infringing AI-generated logo is eligible for trademark registration on the same terms as a human-designed one.

**The substantive challenge: distinctiveness.** The real risk with AI-generated logos is not that the USPTO will reject them for being AI-generated. It is that AI-generated logos may be less distinctive — more generic, more template-like, less memorable — than human-designed logos. This is a quality problem, not a legal one, and it is the strongest argument for using an AI tool like Lovart that supports rich creative iteration rather than accepting the first output.

**The practical challenge: clearance searching.** Before filing a trademark application, you need to search existing marks to confirm your logo is not infringing. This is true for any logo, AI-generated or not. Lovart includes a basic distinctiveness check that compares your generated logo against common visual trademark patterns, but it is not a substitute for a professional trademark search. Budget $500–$2,000 for a comprehensive search and opinion from a trademark attorney before filing.

## International Considerations

The legal landscape varies significantly by jurisdiction. Here is a summary of the key differences as of October 2026:

**European Union:** The EU has taken a more cautious approach to AI-generated works. The EU AI Act, which came into full effect in 2026, requires transparency labeling for AI-generated content but does not bar copyright protection for AI-assisted works. The EUIPO (European Union Intellectual Property Office) has indicated it will evaluate trademark applications for AI-generated logos under the same distinctiveness criteria as human-created logos, without regard to creation method.

**United Kingdom:** The UK is an outlier — Section 9(3) of the CDPA 1988 provides that the "author" of a "computer-generated work" is "the person by whom the arrangements necessary for the creation of the work are undertaken." This potentially offers a statutory basis for protecting AI-generated works that does not exist in U.S. law. However, the UKIPO has been conservative in applying this provision, and case law is sparse.

**China:** Chinese courts have issued conflicting rulings on AI-generated content copyright. A 2024 Beijing Internet Court decision recognized copyright in an AI-generated image where the human user provided detailed prompts and selected among outputs — closely tracking the U.S. "creative control" framework. But the legal situation remains fluid, and the China National Intellectual Property Administration has not issued definitive guidance.

**Practical advice for global brands:** If you are building a brand with international reach, file for trademark protection in your key markets. Trademark law is more harmonized internationally than copyright law, and the creation method of your logo is unlikely to be a barrier in any major jurisdiction.
