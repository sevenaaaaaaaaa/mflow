I shipped 47 packaging designs in Q1 of this year. Coffee bags. Cosmetic bottles. Snack boxes. Candle jars. Tea tins. Different brands, different categories, different price points.

In 2023, 47 packaging designs would have been 6-9 months of work with a team of designers and a structural engineer. In Q1 2026, I did it with one designer (me), AI for concept and visualization, and a print production partner for the physical files.

The AI didn't replace my expertise. It removed the parts of the work that didn't require expertise — the exploration, the iteration, the visualization. The parts where expertise matters — brand judgment, print production knowledge, material selection — I did myself.

Here's the full workflow, the tools, and the 7 things AI can't replace.

## The 5-phase AI packaging workflow

### Phase 1 (2-3 hours): Brand definition

Every packaging project starts with the brand definition. Without it, the AI generates generic packaging. With it, the AI generates brand-consistent packaging.

I use **Lovart's Brand Kit** for this. I define:
- Brand colors (3-5 with hex codes, with WCAG-validated contrast)
- Typography (1-2 typefaces with usage rules)
- Photography/illustration style (3-5 reference images showing the visual language)
- Brand voice adjectives (5-10 descriptors)
- Existing brand assets (logo, secondary marks, pattern libraries)

The Brand Kit becomes the system. Every packaging concept generated downstream inherits this system automatically.

**Time**: 2-3 hours for a new brand. 30 minutes for an existing brand where the Brand Kit is already set up.

### Phase 2 (4-6 hours): Concept exploration

This is where AI shines. I generate 15-30 packaging concepts in an afternoon — variations on the brand applied to the specific package format.

The prompt structure that works:
> "Generate a packaging design for [product]. Package type: [dieline or shape description]. Brand: [brand name]. Brand colors: [hex codes]. Typography: [headline font, body font]. Mood: [3-5 adjectives]. Audience: [target customer]. Required elements: [logo placement, regulatory info, barcode location]. Generate 5 variations. Each should explore a different compositional approach — center-aligned, asymmetric, pattern-based, illustration-focused, type-only."

The AI returns 5 variations per prompt. I run 3-4 prompts (15-20 variations total). I pick the top 3-5 concepts to develop further.

**Time**: 4-6 hours for concept exploration. 30+ concepts generated.

### Phase 3 (1-2 days): Refinement and hero design

The concepts that work become the hero design. The concepts that don't get cut.

I take the top 3-5 concepts into **Lovart's canvas** for refinement. I iterate on:
- Logo placement and sizing
- Headline copy and typography
- Color balance (often the AI gets the palette right but the proportions wrong)
- Regulatory information placement
- Secondary visual elements (patterns, illustrations, icons)
- Material and finish specifications (matte vs gloss, soft-touch vs standard)

For each concept, I generate 3-5 refined variations. The total output of this phase: 3-5 hero design directions ready for production prep.

**Time**: 1-2 days for refinement.

### Phase 4 (1-2 days): Production prep

This is the phase where AI's role shrinks and human expertise takes over.

I take the hero design into **Adobe Illustrator** and apply it to the actual dieline — the unfolded template of the package that the printer uses. Dielines come from the printer or from a packaging-specific template library (Pacdora, Packaging of the World, Jaycee Packaging).

The dieline application requires:
- Understanding bleed (typically 3mm)
- Understanding safe zones (text should stay 3-5mm inside cut lines)
- Understanding panel hierarchy (front, back, sides, top, bottom)
- Understanding print specifications (CMYK color, spot colors if needed, varnish areas, foil areas, embossing areas)
- Understanding barcode and regulatory placement (varies by country and category)

This is where 20+ years of print production experience shows. AI can generate the design. It can't apply it correctly to a dieline because the constraints are physical and technical, not visual.

**Time**: 1-2 days per SKU for production prep.

### Phase 5 (1-2 weeks): Sample rounds and revisions

Once the print-ready file is delivered, the printer produces physical samples. The designer reviews the sample against the original design intent. Revisions are made. New samples are produced.

This phase is unchanged by AI. The samples are physical. The revisions are based on physical material rendering, physical print quality, and physical fit (does the lid close, does the box fold, does the seal work).

**Time**: 1-2 weeks for sample rounds. Unchanged from traditional process.

## Total time and cost

Traditional packaging design (per SKU):
- Brand definition: 1 week
- Concept exploration: 2 weeks
- Refinement: 2 weeks
- Production prep: 1 week
- Sample rounds: 1-2 weeks
- **Total: 7-9 weeks**

AI-assisted packaging design (per SKU):
- Brand definition: 2-3 hours (or 30 min if existing brand)
- Concept exploration: 4-6 hours
- Refinement: 1-2 days
- Production prep: 1-2 days (same human expertise)
- Sample rounds: 1-2 weeks (unchanged)
- **Total: 2-3 weeks**

The AI cuts the front-end design work from 5-6 weeks to 1-3 days. Production prep and sample rounds are unchanged.

For 47 SKUs, the time difference was approximately 6-9 months (traditional) versus 3-4 months (AI-assisted). The savings in cost scale similarly.

## The 7 things AI can't replace

Honest assessment of where AI falls short in packaging design:

**1. Material selection.** AI can show you what a design looks like on kraft paper, but it can't tell you which kraft paper stock your printer stocks, what the minimum order quantity is, or how the material handles moisture and shipping. Material selection requires supplier relationships and physical testing.

**2. Dieline engineering.** The dieline is structural. It's designed for manufacturing, retail, and shipping. AI doesn't understand how a box needs to fold, where the seams need to be, what the closure mechanism looks like. Dieline engineering requires packaging engineering expertise.

**3. Print production specifications.** CMYK conversions, spot color matching, varnish areas, foil stamping, embossing — these are physical print specifications that AI doesn't reliably handle. A design that looks beautiful on screen might print terribly if the specifications are wrong.

**4. Regulatory compliance.** Food packaging has different rules than cosmetics than electronics than pharmaceuticals. Barcode placement, ingredient lists, allergen warnings, recycling symbols, country-of-origin labels — all vary by category and jurisdiction. AI doesn't know the rules.

**5. Physical sample evaluation.** The sample tells you what the printer can actually produce. Color shifts, material behavior, structural issues, finishing quality — all require physical evaluation. AI can show you a mockup. It can't tell you if the seal will hold or if the box will crush in shipping.

**6. Brand judgment.** Is this concept on-brand? Does it feel like the brand? Will it resonate with the target customer? Does it differentiate from competitors? These are judgment calls. AI can generate options. It can't tell you which option is right for the brand.

**7. Client communication.** Explaining the design rationale. Defending choices. Iterating based on feedback. Navigating conflicting feedback from multiple stakeholders. The client relationship is human work that AI doesn't do well.

## When AI packaging design doesn't work

Three scenarios where the AI workflow falls short:

**Completely novel package formats.** If you're designing a package type that doesn't exist yet, AI doesn't have training data to work from. You'll be doing the structural engineering from scratch anyway, and the AI-generated graphics may not fit the novel format.

**Ultra-premium packaging with physical finishes.** Hot foil stamping, embossing, debossing, spot UV, soft-touch laminate — these finishes require physical production and can't be evaluated from a digital file. AI can suggest finishes, but the final decision requires physical samples.

**Heavily regulated categories.** Pharmaceuticals, medical devices, children's products — the regulatory requirements are so specific and consequential that AI generation is risky. The design phase requires regulatory review, not just creative review.

For the 90% of packaging projects that don't fall into these categories — food, beverage, cosmetics, household goods, supplements, candles, apparel, accessories — the AI workflow works well.

## How to talk to clients about AI packaging

The conversation usually starts with "we need packaging design." The right response is "what's the timeline and budget?" Then walk through the AI workflow with the client.

For clients who care about speed and cost: emphasize the time savings (2-3 weeks vs 7-9 weeks) and cost savings (60-80% reduction on design phase).

For clients who care about brand: emphasize that the AI doesn't replace brand judgment — it just accelerates the exploration phase so the brand judgment gets applied to more options.

For clients who care about quality: emphasize that the AI handles concept and visualization, but human expertise handles the structural design, production prep, and sample evaluation. The final output is the same quality as traditional design, just produced faster.

Most clients respond well to this framing. The objections usually come from clients who think AI design looks generic (which is true if the brand definition is weak — the fix is stronger Brand Kit input, not less AI).

## What the 47 designs taught me

Three lessons from shipping 47 packaging designs in one quarter:

**Lesson 1: Brand definition is everything.** The strength of the Brand Kit directly determines the strength of the AI output. Strong Brand Kit → strong AI output. Weak Brand Kit → generic AI output. The time spent on Brand Kit is the highest-ROI time in the entire workflow.

**Lesson 2: Iteration speed changes design choices.** When concept exploration takes 4-6 hours instead of 2 weeks, you explore more directions. You try more compositional approaches. You commit to less quickly. The output is more considered because the exploration was wider.

**Lesson 3: Physical production is still the bottleneck.** The AI didn't reduce the time from "design approved" to "packaged product in hand." Sample rounds, tooling, minimum order quantities — all unchanged. The AI front-end acceleration is real, but the back-end production constraints remain.

## The takeaway

Packaging design used to be a 6-week project with a designer, a printer, and a sample run. The AI workflow cuts the front-end design work to days, with the same or better output quality.

The 7 things AI can't replace — material selection, dieline engineering, print specs, regulatory compliance, sample evaluation, brand judgment, client communication — are exactly the things where expertise matters. Those parts still need humans.

The AI handles the parts where speed matters more than expertise — concept exploration, iteration, visualization. The human handles the parts where expertise matters more than speed — judgment, production, relationships.

For 47 packaging designs in one quarter, this division of labor worked. The next 47 will probably be faster as I refine the Brand Kit process and the prompt templates.

That's the AI packaging design shift. Not replacing designers. Removing the parts of design work that didn't need a designer.

## FAQ

### What is packaging design?
Packaging design is the practice of designing the physical container or wrapper for a product — the structural design (shape, materials, dieline) and the graphic design (logos, typography, color, illustrations, regulatory information). Good packaging protects the product, communicates the brand, complies with regulatory requirements, and stands out on a retail shelf or in an e-commerce thumbnail. It bridges graphic design, industrial design, and print production.

### Can AI design packaging?
Yes, with caveats. AI handles the graphic design phase (concept exploration, color/typography/illustration generation, brand-consistent variants) and the visualization phase (3D mockups, retail-shelf previews) extremely well. AI does not handle structural design (the dieline, the materials engineering, the manufacturing specifications) — that requires CAD tools and human expertise. For graphic design and visualization, AI cuts a 6-week process to 2-3 days. For structural design, AI is still mostly out of scope.

### How long does packaging design take with AI?
Traditional packaging design takes 4-8 weeks: 1-2 weeks for concepts, 2-3 weeks for refinement, 1-2 weeks for production prep (dieline application, print-ready files), 1-2 weeks for sample rounds. AI-assisted packaging design: 1-2 days for concept exploration, 1-2 days for refinement and brand application, 1-2 days for production prep, sample rounds unchanged. Total: 1-2 weeks instead of 4-8. The structural design and sample rounds are unchanged because they require physical production.

### What are the most common packaging design mistakes?
Seven I see repeatedly: (1) Designing for the front only — back panels and side panels need equal attention because they all show on shelf. (2) Ignoring the dieline — designing without considering where folds, seams, and panels actually are. (3) Tiny typography — text that reads on a 24-inch monitor doesn't read at 1-inch on a real package. (4) No shelf context — designing in isolation, not in the retail environment. (5) Ignoring regulatory requirements — barcodes, ingredients, warnings have specific placement rules. (6) Trendy but not ownable — chasing trends that age quickly instead of building a system that lasts. (7) Print-only thinking — designing for print and ignoring how the package looks on a phone screen in e-commerce.

### How much does packaging design cost?
Traditional packaging design: $3,000-15,000 per SKU for a freelance designer, $15,000-50,000+ for an agency. Plus $500-3,000 for sample rounds. Plus the time cost of 4-8 weeks. AI-assisted packaging design: $200-2,000 in AI generation costs per SKU for the front-end design, plus freelance or agency cost for refinement and production prep (typically $1,000-5,000 per SKU). Plus sample rounds unchanged. Plus the time cost of 1-2 weeks. For brands with 10+ SKUs, the savings are substantial — often 60-80% cost reduction on the design phase.
