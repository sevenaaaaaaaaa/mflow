## Episode Overview

Design education is facing its most significant disruption since the shift from analog to digital. When AI can generate logos, layouts, and entire brand systems in seconds, what should design schools teach? Professor Amara Osei has spent the last three years leading RISD's curriculum redesign for the AI era. In this episode, she shares what's working, what's failing, and her candid assessment of which traditional design skills are becoming obsolete — and which matter more than ever.

## Full Transcript

### [00:00] Introduction

**Marcus Chen:** Welcome back to the Lovart Podcast. I'm Marcus Chen. Our first three episodes explored AI design from the perspectives of a design director, an agency founder, and an e-commerce entrepreneur. Today, we're going upstream — to where designers are made. My guest is Professor Amara Osei, a tenured faculty member at the Rhode Island School of Design, where she's been teaching graphic design for 20 years and has been leading the school's effort to redesign its curriculum for the age of AI. Amara, welcome.

**Amara Osei:** Thank you, Marcus. I've been listening to the show. Glad to be here.

**Marcus:** Let's go back to November 2022. ChatGPT launches. Where were you, and what was your first reaction?

**Amara:** I was in my office at RISD, grading sophomore typography projects. A colleague burst in and said, "You need to see this." She showed me ChatGPT generating passable design rationales — the kind of written justifications we spend weeks teaching students to articulate. My first reaction was pure denial. "It's a parlor trick. It can't do real design work." Within six months, I was completely wrong, and I knew it.

### [07:15] The Great Debate

**Marcus:** Design schools had fierce debates about whether students should be allowed to use AI. Where did RISD land?

**Amara:** It was — and still is — the most contentious faculty debate I've ever been part of. The arguments broke into three camps.

**The Ban Camp:** "AI is plagiarism. It steals from artists. Students using AI aren't learning design — they're learning to prompt. Ban it from every classroom."

**The Embrace Camp:** "AI is the future of the profession. Students who graduate without AI fluency will be unemployable. Integrate it into every course immediately."

**The Middle Camp:** "AI is a tool. Like any tool, when and how you use it matters. Teach students to use AI thoughtfully, not to depend on it."

RISD landed — after about a year of extremely heated faculty meetings — in the middle camp. Our official policy: AI is permitted as a tool in the design process, but not as a substitute for developing fundamental skills. Students must be able to articulate what the AI is doing and why. They must demonstrate critical judgment over AI outputs. And in foundational courses — typography, color theory, composition — AI is restricted for specific assignments to ensure students develop core competencies.

**Marcus:** How do you enforce that? How do you know if a student used AI?

**Amara:** Honestly? We don't always know. And that's part of why the "ban" position was ultimately untenable. You can't effectively ban a tool that students can access on their phones. So instead of policing, we've shifted to designing assignments that make AI usage visible and educational rather than hidden and cheating.

For example, in my advanced branding course, students are required to submit their prompt history alongside their final designs. The assignment isn't "create a brand identity" — it's "use AI to explore 50 brand identity directions, document your curation process, and develop the most promising 3 directions into complete systems." The AI usage is the assignment, not a shortcut around it.

### [12:00] What We're Keeping

**Marcus:** What parts of the traditional design curriculum are you keeping unchanged?

**Amara:** Three areas remain essentially AI-proof in our curriculum:

**Typography fundamentals.** AI can generate type, but it doesn't understand why one typeface communicates trust and another communicates playfulness. Students still need to learn the history of type, the anatomy of letterforms, and the emotional impact of typographic choices. We still teach them to set type by hand — not because they'll ever do it professionally, but because the physical act of setting type builds an intuitive understanding that purely digital work doesn't.

**Color theory and perception.** AI can generate beautiful color palettes, but it doesn't understand cultural color connotations, accessibility implications, or the psychology of color interaction. Our color theory sequence remains largely unchanged — we've actually added content on AI color systems to help students understand what the AI is doing under the hood.

**Design history and criticism.** Understanding why design movements emerged, what problems they were solving, and how to critique visual work remains essential. AI has no historical consciousness. It can simulate the Bauhaus style, but it doesn't understand what the Bauhaus was reacting against or why it mattered. That contextual understanding is what separates a designer from a decorator — and what separates valuable human creative direction from generic AI output.

### [16:45] What We're Removing

**Marcus:** And what's getting cut or significantly reduced?

**Amara:** We're dramatically reducing the time spent on software instruction. Ten years ago, a significant portion of the sophomore year was learning Adobe Creative Suite — how to use the pen tool, how to set up master pages, how to prepare files for print. All of that is being compressed. Students still need to understand file formats, color spaces, and production processes — but they don't need to spend 40 hours learning Photoshop when AI tools can execute those technical operations on natural language instruction.

We're also reducing repetitive production assignments. The old model of "design this poster in 50 variations to learn about iteration" is being replaced with "generate 200 variations with AI, curate to 10, and explain your curation decisions." The learning outcome — understanding what makes one design work better than another — is the same or better. But the time spent on mechanical production is dramatically reduced.

And we've eliminated some technical courses entirely. Our "Digital Production Techniques" course — which taught file optimization, batch processing, and production workflows — has been replaced with "AI-Augmented Production," which covers Lovart batch generation, automated asset pipelines, and AI-powered quality assurance.

### [21:30] New Courses

**Marcus:** What new courses have you created?

**Amara:** We've introduced four new required courses in the past two years:

**"Prompt Engineering for Designers"** — Not just "how to write prompts," but how to think critically about what you're asking the AI to do. Students learn to structure creative briefs as prompt frameworks, to layer instructions for iterative refinement, and to recognize when AI is giving them what they asked for versus what they actually need.

**"AI, Ethics, and Design"** — Covers the legal and ethical dimensions of AI design: copyright, fair use, bias in training data, labor displacement, environmental impact of AI computation, and the designer's ethical obligations when using AI tools.

**"Systems Design for AI"** — How to design brand systems that are optimized for AI generation. The old model was designing individual outputs. The new model is designing systems that produce consistent outputs at scale. This course teaches students to think in terms of rules, constraints, and parameters rather than specific visual solutions.

**"Design + Data"** — How to use performance data to inform visual decisions. Students connect their designs to analytics platforms and learn to interpret which visual choices drive which outcomes. This course exists because AI makes it possible to generate and test hundreds of design variants — but you need data literacy to know what to learn from those tests.

### [26:00] Student Reactions

**Marcus:** How are students responding to all this change?

**Amara:** It's complicated. There's genuine anxiety — "Am I spending $60,000 a year on a degree that AI will make obsolete?" There's excitement — students who embrace AI are producing work at a level I've never seen from undergraduates. And there's ethical angst — students who feel morally conflicted about using tools trained on uncompensated artist data.

The students who are thriving share a common trait: they see AI as a collaborator, not a replacement. They use AI for exploration and production, but they maintain strong creative opinions. They know what they want to say, and they use AI to help them say it better. The students who are struggling tend to approach AI passively — they prompt, accept whatever comes out, and don't develop their own creative judgment.

That passive approach is what we're trying hardest to prevent. Because the passive AI user — the one who accepts whatever the machine generates — will absolutely be replaced by AI. The active AI user — the one with strong taste, strategic thinking, and the ability to direct and refine AI output — will be more valuable than ever.

### [30:15] Skills That Matter More Than Ever

**Marcus:** What skills do you tell students are becoming more important, not less?

**Amara:** Taste. Taste is everything. The ability to look at 50 designs and instantly identify the 3 that are worth pursuing — that's not something AI can do. It's built through thousands of hours of looking at design, analyzing why things work, and developing an internal quality compass. We're doubling down on taste development through curated reference libraries, critique sessions, and exposure to the best design across cultures and time periods.

**Writing.** We require all design students to take additional writing courses now. The interface for AI design tools is natural language. The quality of your output is directly proportional to the quality of your prompts — and the quality of your prompts is directly proportional to your ability to articulate creative vision in words. A designer who can't write well in 2027 is like a designer who couldn't draw well in 1997. It's becoming a core competency.

**Critical thinking.** AI can generate design options. It cannot tell you which option aligns with the brand strategy, which option will resonate with the target audience, or which option is ethically appropriate for the context. Those decisions require human judgment informed by research, empathy, and ethical reasoning.

**Collaboration.** Design is increasingly a conversation between humans and AI. Students need to learn how to give AI effective direction, how to interpret AI outputs critically, and how to iterate productively rather than accept passively. This is a new skill — neither traditional design education nor traditional technology education teaches it.

### [37:00] Advice for Aspiring Designers

**Marcus:** Final question. An 18-year-old is considering design school in 2027. What do you tell them?

**Amara:** I'd tell them: the job title "designer" is being deconstructed. In ten years, there won't be "designers" in the traditional sense — people who spend their days moving pixels around a canvas. There will be creative directors, brand strategists, design system architects, AI design specialists, and visual data analysts.

The core skill isn't going to be "making things look good." It's going to be "knowing what good looks like and being able to direct AI to produce it." That's a fundamentally different skill set, and it's one that can be taught — but it requires different training than what design schools were offering five years ago.

If you're considering design school, ask hard questions about the curriculum. "How are you teaching AI? Is it banned or integrated? Are you teaching prompt engineering? Are you teaching data-informed design? Are you preparing students for the jobs that will exist in 2030, not the jobs that existed in 2010?" If the school can't answer those questions convincingly, look elsewhere.

And regardless of whether you go to design school or not — develop your taste. Look at thousands of designs. Figure out why the best ones work. Build a mental library of references across design history, across cultures, across industries. That library is your irreplaceable competitive advantage. AI can't replicate it, and it's what will make your creative direction valuable even as the tools become more powerful.

**Marcus:** Professor Amara Osei from RISD. Thank you for a fascinating conversation.

**Amara:** Thank you, Marcus. It's an important conversation. I'm glad we're having it publicly.

**Marcus:** That's Episode 4 of the Lovart Podcast. Join us next time for our season finale. Subscribe so you don't miss it.
