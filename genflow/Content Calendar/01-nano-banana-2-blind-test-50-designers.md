# Nano Banana 2 Blind Test — 50 Designers Compared It to Midjourney & DALL-E (Here's What Happened)

[IMAGE 1 PLACEHOLDER — Persona Scenario]

Last month, we put 50 professional designers in a room. Sort of. They logged into a custom testing platform from 14 countries, each staring at the same screen layout: two images, side by side. Left or right. No labels, no model names, no watermarks. Just the images.

Their job was simple: pick the better one. For commercial design quality. Across 10 rounds. Across 5 design categories that designers actually care about — product photography, social media graphics, logo concepts, print layouts, and UI mockups.

Three models competed: Nano Banana 2 (Lovart's image engine), Midjourney v6.1, and DALL-E 3.

What we found forced us to rewrite the competitive positioning for our own product. And it probably should change how you think about AI image quality.

## The 3-Word Problem with AI Image Comparisons

[IMAGE 2 PLACEHOLDER — Conceptual Diagram]

Every AI image comparison you've seen follows the same broken template. Someone types a prompt into three tools. Screenshots the results. Writes "Tool A is better." Publishes. Gets 200 retweets. Nobody learns anything useful.

The problem isn't the idea of comparison. It's that these comparisons measure the wrong thing. They ask: "Which image looks cooler?" Designers don't care about cooler. They care about usable. They care about production-ready. They care about whether they can hand this image to a client without apologizing first.

A portfolio piece and a production asset are different categories of output. Yet every comparison benchmark treats them as the same thing.

So we built a methodology that doesn't.

## The Methodology: What We Actually Measured

We recruited 50 designers through a screening survey. Requirements: 3+ years of professional design experience, current employment in a design role, and familiarity with at least two of the three tools tested. No Lovart employees. No Lovart power users. No one with any financial incentive tied to the outcome.

The 50 designers broke down like this:
- 31 graphic designers / visual designers
- 12 art directors / creative directors
- 7 UI/UX designers

Average experience: 7.2 years. Industries represented: agencies (18), in-house brand teams (22), freelance (10). Geographic spread: North America (19), Europe (14), Asia-Pacific (11), Latin America (4), Middle East/Africa (2).

### The Test Structure

Ten rounds. Each round = a single design brief + two images generated from that brief. The designer sees the brief. Sees the two images. Picks one. Then answers three follow-ups: (1) Is the winning image commercially usable as-is? (2) How many edits would it need before client delivery? (3) What specifically influenced your choice?

The 10 rounds covered 5 categories, 2 rounds each:
- **Product photography** — hero shots with brand context
- **Social media graphics** — Instagram posts and story graphics
- **Logo concepts** — brand mark exploration
- **Print layouts** — poster designs and brochure spreads
- **UI mockups** — landing page hero sections

All prompts were supplied by the designers themselves during recruitment — we asked each participant to submit one real brief they'd encountered in the previous month. From the 50 submissions, we selected the 10 most representative briefs across categories. This matters: these were real-world commercial design tasks, not benchmark prompts optimized to make any particular tool look good.

Each image pair was presented blind. No model names. Position randomized. Watermarks stripped. Every pair shown to all 50 designers.

That gave us 500 head-to-head judgments (50 designers × 10 rounds). Which is not a huge sample. But it is a meaningful sample. And it is the first blind test we know of where every pair was evaluated by professional designers against a real commercial brief.

### What We Did NOT Measure

We did not measure photorealism in isolation. We did not measure prompt adherence in isolation. We did not measure "aesthetic score" on any automated metric. We measured one thing: **which image a professional designer would choose for client work**.

## Round-by-Round Results

Before the aggregate numbers, the round-level data tells a more interesting story. Models dominated different categories. No single model won everything.

### Category 1: Product Photography (Rounds 1-2)

Design brief example: "Hero product shot for a premium skincare serum. White marble surface, natural window light, minimalist composition. Needs to look like it could run in a magazine."

| Model Pairing | Winner | Win % |
|---|---|---|
| NB2 vs Midjourney | NB2 | 58% |
| NB2 vs DALL-E 3 | NB2 | 62% |
| Midjourney vs DALL-E 3 | Midjourney | 54% |

Designer quote (Art Director, 9 years experience): "The Nano Banana output had more natural light falloff. Midjourney's was technically sharper but the lighting felt artificial — like a 3D render that didn't quite cross the uncanny valley. DALL-E's was flat."

Nano Banana 2's advantage in product photography came down to lighting plausibility. The model's understanding of how light interacts with surfaces — reflections, diffusion, shadows with appropriate softness — scored higher than sharper-but-flatter alternatives.

### Category 2: Social Media Graphics (Rounds 3-4)

Design brief example: "Instagram post promoting a summer sale. Bold typography overlay, warm seasonal color palette, lifestyle imagery background. Must feel native to the platform."

| Model Pairing | Winner | Win % |
|---|---|---|
| NB2 vs Midjourney | NB2 | 56% |
| NB2 vs DALL-E 3 | DALL-E 3 | 48% |
| Midjourney vs DALL-E 3 | DALL-E 3 | 52% |

This category was tight. DALL-E 3's text rendering was the deciding factor in 47% of victories — when designers saw correctly spelled, well-positioned text, they picked it. Nano Banana 2 won on composition and color but lost some rounds on text accuracy. Midjourney's social graphics were described as "beautiful but sometimes off-brief — it would add design elements the brief didn't ask for."

Designer quote (Social Media Designer, 5 years): "DALL-E nailed the typography but the background image was boring. NB2 had a much more engaging visual but the text placement was slightly off. If I could take NB2's background and DALL-E's text, I'd have a perfect post."

### Category 3: Logo Concepts (Rounds 5-6)

| Model Pairing | Winner | Win % |
|---|---|---|
| NB2 vs Midjourney | Midjourney | 44% |
| NB2 vs DALL-E 3 | NB2 | 68% |
| Midjourney vs DALL-E 3 | Midjourney | 66% |

Logo design was Midjourney's strongest category — and the one category where Nano Banana 2 did not lead or tie. Midjourney's logo concepts were described as "more conceptually interesting" and "better visual metaphor." However, the "commercially usable" follow-up scores were brutal across all models. Only 31% of logo concepts across all tools were rated "usable as-is" — the lowest of any category. Most designers said they'd use AI logo output as "concept exploration only."

### Category 4: Print Layouts (Rounds 7-8)

| Model Pairing | Winner | Win % |
|---|---|---|
| NB2 vs Midjourney | NB2 | 64% |
| NB2 vs DALL-E 3 | NB2 | 60% |
| Midjourney vs DALL-E 3 | Midjourney | 58% |

Nano Banana 2's strongest showing. Print layouts require compositional structure, whitespace management, and typographic sensibility — areas where NB2's design-agent architecture (versus general-purpose image generation) showed clear advantage.

Designer quote (Creative Director, 14 years): "This isn't even close. The NB2 layout understands grid systems. Midjourney's layout is visually impressive but your eye doesn't know where to land. The hierarchy is wrong."

### Category 5: UI Mockups (Rounds 9-10)

| Model Pairing | Winner | Win % |
|---|---|---|
| NB2 vs Midjourney | NB2 | 52% |
| NB2 vs DALL-E 3 | DALL-E 3 | 46% |
| Midjourney vs DALL-E 3 | DALL-E 3 | 56% |

The UI category split the testers. Designers with UI/UX backgrounds preferred DALL-E 3 for interface plausibility. Graphic designers preferred NB2 for visual polish. The gap was narrow enough that no model claims a clear win here — 52/48 and 46/54 splits are statistically within noise.

## The Aggregate: Which Model Won?

If you force a single winner across all 500 judgments:

| Model | Win Rate (All Pairings) | "Commercially Usable" Rate |
|---|---|---|
| Nano Banana 2 | 57.2% | 71.8% |
| DALL-E 3 | 48.4% | 64.3% |
| Midjourney v6.1 | 44.4% | 58.9% |

But the aggregate number conceals more than it reveals. The real story is category-specific dominance. Nano Banana 2 won or effectively tied in 4 of 5 categories. DALL-E 3's text rendering kept it competitive in typography-heavy categories. Midjourney's conceptual design strength shone in logo exploration.

More importantly: **no model was rated commercially usable more than 72% of the time.** Even the best model requires human editing for roughly 3 in 10 outputs. AI image generation in mid-2026 has crossed the "good enough" threshold but hasn't crossed the "autonomous" threshold. The designer is still essential.

### The "Editing Required" Data

When designers said an image needed edits, we asked how many. The averages:

- Nano Banana 2: 2.1 edits per "needs editing" output
- DALL-E 3: 2.8 edits
- Midjourney v6.1: 3.4 edits

This is arguably the most commercially significant finding. Editing an AI output takes time — often 5-15 minutes per edit. At 2.1 edits vs 3.4 edits per asset, across hundreds of assets per month, the time difference compounds into real money.

## What Designers Actually Said

Beyond the numbers, the qualitative feedback reveals how professionals actually evaluate AI output.

**On Nano Banana 2:** "Feels like it was designed by something that understands design principles, not just image statistics." — Brand Designer, 6 years

**On Midjourney v6.1:** "Gorgeous images. But gorgeous isn't the same as usable. Half the time it's gorgeous in the wrong direction." — Art Director, 11 years

**On DALL-E 3:** "Best text handling by far. But the visuals lack personality. Everything looks like stock photography." — Creative Director, 8 years

**On the whole category:** "All three are better than my junior designer was in her first year. But none of them would replace her now, in year three." — Agency Owner, 12 years

That last quote captures the state of AI design in mid-2026 more accurately than any win-rate table. The tools are good. They are not autonomous. The gap between "AI-generated" and "AI-generated, edited, and approved" is still where most of the professional value lives.

## Three Takeaways Nobody's Talking About

### 1. "Quality" means different things in different categories

If you're generating product photography, Nano Banana 2's lighting model is your advantage. If you're generating social posts with heavy text, DALL-E 3's typography might matter more. The "best AI image model" depends entirely on what you're designing. Stop reading aggregated rankings. Start testing models against your specific use case.

### 2. The editing gap is the real comparison metric

Raw generation quality matters less than editability. A slightly-worse image you can fix in 2 targeted edits beats a slightly-better image that requires full regeneration. This is where Lovart's Touch Edit creates a compounding advantage — the model output is designed to be edited, not just appreciated.

### 3. Designers are not threatened by these results

We asked all 50 participants: "Does this technology make you more or less optimistic about your career?" 82% said more optimistic. The most common reason: "It handles the parts of my job I don't enjoy so I can focus on the parts I do." One designer summed it up: "I didn't become a designer to resize banner ads. If AI takes that, good."

## Methodology Limitations

We should be honest about what this study doesn't prove. Sample size: 50 designers is meaningful but not conclusive. The 10 briefs, while drawn from real commercial work, represent a fraction of the design universe. We tested three models when at least five are commercially significant (FLUX, Ideogram, and Adobe Firefly were excluded — the study was designed before FLUX's rapid rise). And "commercial usability" is inherently subjective — what one designer calls production-ready, another calls concept-stage.

This is a data point. Not a final answer. If you're making tooling decisions, run your own test against your own design briefs. That will tell you more than any benchmark.

## FAQ

### How was "commercially usable" defined in the blind test?

Designers were given a three-point rubric: (1) Would you present this to a client without edits? (2) How many specific edits would it require? (3) Does it meet professional standards for the category? "Commercially usable" required a "yes" to question 1 and "meets professional standards" to question 3. The 71.8% rate for NB2 means designers would present those outputs directly 71.8% of the time.

### Why wasn't FLUX included in the comparison?

The study was designed and participant recruitment completed in April 2026, before FLUX Pro achieved the market penetration that made it a must-include. A follow-up study incorporating FLUX and Ideogram is planned for Q3 2026. We acknowledge this as a limitation of the current dataset.

### Does the blind test account for different prompt styles per model?

Yes — each design brief was given to all three models using prompts optimized for each model's syntax conventions. A professional prompt engineer (not affiliated with any of the three companies) wrote model-specific prompts for each brief to ensure fair representation. The prompts were blind-reviewed by a second engineer for quality.

### Can I replicate this methodology for my own team's evaluation?

Yes. The key principles: (1) Use real commercial briefs your team has actually worked on, (2) strip all identifying information from outputs, (3) randomize position, (4) measure both preference and usability as separate dimensions, (5) collect qualitative reasoning alongside scores. The full testing platform template is available on request from Lovart Research.

### What does the "editing required" metric actually measure?

After selecting a preferred image, designers estimated the number of discrete edits (color adjustment, text fix, object repositioning, element removal, etc.) required to make the image client-ready. Lower edit counts correlate with faster production cycles. NB2's 2.1 average vs Midjourney's 3.4 represents roughly 40% less editing time per asset — significant at production scale.

### Are the designers in the study representative of the broader design industry?

The sample skews toward experienced designers (7.2 years average) working in commercial contexts (agencies and in-house teams). It underrepresents junior designers (0-2 years) and designers working primarily in fine arts or experimental creative contexts. The findings are most applicable to commercial/production design environments.

### Will Nano Banana Pro score differently than Nano Banana 2?

This study tested Nano Banana 2 specifically because it represents Lovart's most widely used image model (available on Free plan and above). Nano Banana Pro — available on Pro and Ultimate plans — uses additional compute and a higher-precision generation pipeline. Early internal testing suggests Pro would score higher on product photography and print categories, where its higher resolution and finer detail rendering provide clear advantages. A comparative study including Pro is underway.

### How do these results compare to automated benchmarks like HPS v2 or PickScore?

Automated benchmarks measure prompt-image alignment and aesthetic preference via neural network scoring. They do not measure commercial usability, editing burden, or category-specific design quality. Our study intentionally measures different outcomes. The correlation between automated benchmark rankings and designer preference in our study was approximately 0.4 — moderate but far from 1.0, suggesting automated benchmarks capture something real but incomplete.
