# AI Design Disclosure: Should You Tell Clients You Used AI?

[IMAGE 1 PLACEHOLDER — Persona Scenario]

A designer sits down to send a client deliverable. The work is solid. The layout is clean. The brand is respected. But there's a knot in their stomach because Lovart handled 70% of the production. Should they mention it?

This isn't a hypothetical. It's the conversation happening in every design Slack community, freelance forum, and agency standup right now. And it's more nuanced than the hot takes suggest.

Let's work through it honestly.

## The Two Disclosure Camps (and Why Both Miss the Point)

[IMAGE 2 PLACEHOLDER — Conceptual Diagram]

The AI disclosure debate has largely split into two positions:

**Camp 1: "Always disclose. It's unethical not to."**
The argument: clients are paying for human expertise. If AI did the work, they deserve to know. Anything less is deception.

**Camp 2: "Never disclose. You don't detail every tool you use."**
The argument: you don't tell clients which Figma plugin you used, which stock photo site you searched, or which color picker extension you installed. AI is just another tool in the stack. Disclosing it invites unnecessary scrutiny and devalues your work.

Both camps are wrong — not because their values are wrong, but because they're treating disclosure as a binary answer to what's actually a contextual question.

The right question isn't "should I disclose?" — it's "what does this client need to know to make informed decisions about the work I'm delivering?"

## The Framework: Disclose Process, Not Tools

Here's a principle that cuts through the noise: **clients care about outcomes and integrity, not your software stack.**

A client who hires you for a logo doesn't need to know whether you sketched thumbnails in Procreate, refined vectors in Illustrator, or generated concepts in Lovart. They need to know the work is original, the files are theirs, and the result serves their brand.

A client who hires you for ongoing social media production at $2,000/month needs to understand your capacity and deliverables. If AI enables you to produce 40 posts a month where a purely manual process would yield 15, that's a capacity conversation worth having — because it shapes expectations, pricing, and the relationship itself.

The distinction: **disclose what affects the client's decisions. Don't itemize your toolchain.**

This aligns with how other professions handle technology disclosure. Your accountant doesn't disclose that they used Excel macros to run your projections. Your lawyer doesn't disclose which legal research database they queried. The tool matters less than the judgment applied to it.

## When Disclosure Is the Right Call

[IMAGE 3 PLACEHOLDER — Real UI Screenshot]

There are situations where proactively discussing AI use strengthens your client relationship:

### 1. When AI Enables the Relationship to Exist

A small business that couldn't afford a $5,000 branding package but can afford your $1,500 AI-accelerated version deserves to understand why the price works. "I use AI for initial concept generation and production, which lets me offer branding services at a price point that makes sense for your stage" is honest, clear, and positions AI as an accessibility enabler, not a quality compromise.

### 2. When Speed Is a Deliverable

If a client needs a campaign turned around in 24 hours and your AI workflow makes that possible, transparency builds trust: "I can hit this deadline because my production process uses AI to accelerate layout and variations. The creative direction and final quality control are still 100% me." The client gains confidence in your ability to deliver under pressure.

### 3. When the Client Has an Ethical Stake

Some clients — nonprofits with donor transparency commitments, B Corps with ethical supply chain standards, brands with "human-made" positioning — may have a legitimate need to understand the role of AI in their creative production. Withholding that information could expose them to reputational risk they didn't consent to. In these cases, disclosure is an ethical obligation.

### 4. When Ownership and Rights Are at Stake

If your AI tool's terms of service claim any rights over outputs, your client needs to know. (Lovart doesn't — customer outputs are fully owned by the customer, with no usage restrictions.) But the broader point stands: any factor that affects the client's intellectual property rights must be disclosed.

### 5. When the Client Asks Directly

This one is obvious but worth stating: if a client asks "do you use AI in your process?" — answer honestly. Trust lost on a direct question is nearly impossible to rebuild.

## When Disclosure Is Unnecessary (or Even Unhelpful)

Equally important: recognizing when disclosure serves no one.

### When It Confuses More Than It Clarifies

Explaining MCoT (Multi-step Chain of Thought) prompting and Touch Edit workflows to a client who just wants to know if their Facebook ad looks good is not transparency — it's noise. Don't mistake technical detail for ethical communication.

### When It Undermines Perceived Value Without Reason

A client who loves the work, renews the contract, and refers you to peers doesn't benefit from learning that AI was involved — unless that knowledge changes something material about the relationship. Introducing doubt where none existed isn't ethical; it's self-sabotage.

### When AI Is Used for Trivial Production Tasks

Resizing a design for six platform formats with Lovart's resize command is functionally identical to doing it manually in Photoshop — just faster. You wouldn't disclose that you used "Transform > Scale" instead of redrawing an asset pixel by pixel. The same logic applies.

## How to Frame the Conversation When You Do Disclose

If you decide disclosure makes sense, how you frame it determines whether the client sees AI as an asset or a liability.

**Don't say:** "I used AI to design this."

**Say:** "I used AI tools to accelerate the production process — generating layout options, handling format variations, and building out the template system. Every creative decision — concept, color, typography, composition — was mine."

The difference: the first framing positions AI as the creator. The second positions AI as the production accelerator. One dilutes your value. The other amplifies it.

**Don't say:** "AI did most of the work, I just tweaked it."

**Say:** "I directed the AI through a series of specific creative prompts based on our strategy sessions, then refined the outputs to match your brand precisely."

The difference: the first framing suggests you phoned it in. The second shows you orchestrated a sophisticated creative process.

## The Bottom Line

AI design disclosure isn't a moral absolute — it's a professional judgment call that depends on context, client relationship, and the nature of the work.

The principle: **tell clients what they need to know to make informed decisions. Use your professional judgment to determine what that is.** Don't hide information that affects their rights, their reputation, or their strategy. Don't volunteer information that adds confusion without adding value.

And if you're ever unsure, ask yourself: "If this client found out a year from now that I used AI in this project, would they feel deceived or impressed?" Let that be your guide.
