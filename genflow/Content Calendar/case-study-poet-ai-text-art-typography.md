# How Poet Amara Diallo Turned 83 Poems into Visual Art — And Built a Gallery Exhibition Without Touching a Paintbrush

[IMAGE 1 PLACEHOLDER — Persona Scenario]

Amara Diallo's poetry collection, *What the Water Held*, had been a critical success. Published by a respected independent press in Dakar, it had earned two literary prizes, a nomination for the Brunel Prize, and reviews that praised its "luminous, liquid language" and "visceral sense of place."

What it hadn't earned was a visual audience.

"The people who read poetry are a small, devoted community. The people who look at art — who walk through galleries, who share images, who respond to visual beauty — are a much larger community. My words were reaching one and missing the other entirely."

Amara had always imagined her poems as visual experiences — the imagery was specific, painterly, rooted in the West African coastal landscapes of her childhood. But her visual-arts skills were limited to enthusiastic amateurism. The paintings she'd attempted to accompany her readings were, in her own assessment, "embarrassing."

She discovered that AI could translate poetic language into visual art with startling fidelity — and that her gift for imagery was, in effect, a gift for art direction. Eighty-three poems became 83 visual works. The exhibition, *What the Water Held: Poems Made Visible*, sold out at Galerie Yoff in Dakar. Amara is now commissioned by a museum to create a permanent installation.

## The Background

[IMAGE 2 PLACEHOLDER — Conceptual Diagram]

Amara, 32, is a Senegalese poet whose work explores memory, water, migration, and the Atlantic coast where she grew up. Her poetry is vividly visual — descriptions of fishermen at dawn, the texture of wet sand, the light through harmattan haze, the colors of traditional fabric against dark skin — but her medium was exclusively text.

"I've always seen my poems. The words were a way of recording what I saw in my mind. But readers couldn't see what I saw — they could only read about it. I wanted to close that gap. I wanted people to see the images I'd been describing all along."

The limitations were practical, not conceptual. Amara knew exactly what her poems looked like. She couldn't paint them, couldn't afford to commission 83 paintings ($3,000-15,000 each), and couldn't accept that her visual vision would remain forever trapped in text.

## The Pain Points

### 1. The Text-Image Translation Gap

Poetry and visual art are treated as separate disciplines with separate audiences, separate institutions, and separate funding streams. Poets who want to create visual work face a structural barrier: the skills that make someone an exceptional poet (language, rhythm, metaphor, compression) don't translate to visual execution (composition, color theory, technique, material knowledge). The gap isn't creative failure — it's a category error. Expecting a poet to also be a painter is like expecting a novelist to also be a filmmaker. Some can do both; most can't; the assumption that they should limits cross-medium creativity.

### 2. The Exhibition Economics

A gallery exhibition of 83 works — even modestly sized at 40x50cm prints — costs $8,000-25,000 to produce through traditional means (artist commissions, printing, framing, installation). For a poet whose annual income from writing was roughly $12,000, this was functionally impossible. The economics of poetry and the economics of visual art operated at entirely different scales — and a poet who wanted to cross over had no bridge.

### 3. The Audience Fragmentation

West African literary audiences and West African visual-art audiences overlap minimally. Amara's poetry readers — a community of perhaps 3,000-5,000 across Francophone Africa and the diaspora — had almost no intersection with the gallery-going public in Dakar. The exhibition wasn't just about making art; it was about building a bridge between two audiences who didn't know they shared an artist.

## The Turning Point

Amara discovered Lovart through a friend who used AI for book-cover design. She was initially skeptical — she'd seen AI-generated "poetry visuals" that looked generic, decorative, unrelated to the specific imagery of the poems they were supposedly illustrating.

Her test: she took one of her most visually specific poems — a piece about her grandmother washing clothes in the Atlantic surf at dawn, the fabric colors bleeding into the foam — and described the image to Lovart in the same language she'd used in the poem.

Prompt: "West African grandmother washing brightly colored fabric in Atlantic surf at dawn, the colors bleeding into white foam — deep indigo, burnt orange, emerald green. The light is golden and low, harmattan haze softening the horizon. The figures are small against the ocean. The feeling is ancestral, elemental, sacred and ordinary at the same time. Fine art photography aesthetic, not illustration."

The AI generated an image that captured the poem's visual essence — not a literal illustration of the text, but a visual work that communicated the same emotional and sensory experience. The colors bled. The light was golden. The figures were small against the ocean. The feeling was right.

"I looked at the image and thought: someone who had never read my poem could look at this and feel what the poem feels. That's translation. Not word-to-image translation. Feeling-to-image translation. I immediately generated 10 more."

## The Solution: Amara's Poem-to-Image Process

### Translation, Not Illustration

Amara's critical insight: she wasn't illustrating her poems. She was translating their visual essence into a different medium. The prompt wasn't "draw what the poem describes." It was "generate an image that feels the way this poem feels."

The difference is fundamental:
- **Illustration** shows what the poem says — literal, descriptive, one-to-one mapping
- **Translation** evokes what the poem feels — emotional, interpretive, sensory resonance

Amara chose translation. Each of the 83 visual works was generated from the poem's emotional and sensory core, not its literal content. The result was a body of work that stood independently as visual art while maintaining a deep, non-literal connection to the poetry.

### The Exhibition Design

The exhibition was deliberately structured as a dialogue between text and image:
- Each visual work was displayed alongside its source poem, printed on the gallery wall
- Visitors could read the poem, look at the image, and experience the relationship between them
- A QR code next to each piece linked to an audio recording of Amara reading the poem — adding the dimension of spoken language

The tripartite experience (text + image + voice) created something that neither a poetry reading nor an art exhibition could offer alone: a complete sensory encounter with the work.

### The Social Bridge

The exhibition opening brought together two audiences who rarely occupied the same room: Dakar's literary community (writers, critics, publishers) and its visual-art community (gallery owners, collectors, artists, curators). The cross-pollination was immediate and productive — writers discovered visual art, artists discovered poetry, and Amara was suddenly visible to both communities simultaneously.

## FAQ

### Can AI translate poetry into visual art that actually captures the poem's meaning?

AI can translate the visual and sensory dimensions of poetry — imagery, color, texture, light, composition — into images that evoke the poem's emotional register. It cannot translate abstract conceptual meaning, linguistic play, or structural poetic devices. The translation is sensory and emotional, not semantic and literal. Amara's approach — translating how the poem feels rather than what it says — is the effective approach. Expecting AI to "illustrate" a poem's meaning will produce disappointment. Expecting it to "visualize" a poem's atmosphere will produce revelation.

### How do I prompt AI to translate a poem into an image?

Amara's method: (1) Identify the poem's core sensory elements — colors, textures, light, scale, movement, space; (2) Identify the poem's emotional register — the feeling it produces, described in visual terms ("ancestral and elemental," "intimate and fragile," "vast and indifferent"); (3) Combine sensory and emotional elements into a prompt: "West African grandmother washing fabric in surf at dawn. Golden harmattan light. Colors bleeding into foam. Small figures against vast ocean. Ancestral, sacred, ordinary. Fine art photography." The goal is visual specificity with emotional resonance — not description of the poem's content, but evocation of the poem's atmosphere.

### Can AI-generated visual poetry be considered "art" in the traditional sense?

This question drives critical discourse but misses the operational reality: Amara's works sold at a gallery exhibition to collectors who considered them art. The museum commissioning her installation considers it art. The audiences who experienced the text-image-voice tripartite works considered them art. Whether something "is art" is determined by how it functions in the art ecosystem — not by critical taxonomy. AI-assisted work that functions as art (exhibited, collected, commissioned, discussed) is art in every practically meaningful sense.

### How do you price AI-generated visual poetry for gallery sales?

Amara's pricing model: limited editions of 5 prints per image, priced at €250-600 depending on size and exhibition context. The pricing reflects production cost (irrelevant — near-zero), exhibition context (gallery validation), artist reputation (prize-winning poet + exhibition track record), and edition scarcity. The price point was deliberately accessible (below the €1,000+ "serious collector" threshold) to encourage purchases from poetry readers who had never bought visual art — and it worked; 41% of buyers were first-time art purchasers.

### Can I exhibit AI-generated visual poetry without the text alongside?

Amara exhibited text alongside image because the dialogue between them was central to the exhibition concept. The images work independently as visual art, but they gain depth when experienced in relationship to the source poems. Whether to include text depends on your artistic intention: if the images are translations of poems, include the poems. If the poems were merely prompts and the images are the primary artwork, the poems are optional. Amara's approach — text and image in dialogue — was the exhibition's conceptual core.

### What print specifications work best for AI-generated visual poetry?

Amara's exhibition specifications: archival pigment prints on Hahnemühle Photo Rag 308gsm (matte fine-art paper), printed at 40x50cm (for intimate viewing distance), framed in simple black wood with UV-protective glass. The matte paper and black frame aesthetic signaled "fine art" rather than "photo reproduction" to gallery visitors. Print quality is essential — AI generation produces the image; professional printing makes it gallery-worthy.

### Can musicians and other non-visual artists use this approach?

The translation principle extends beyond poetry: musicians can translate songs into visual works (what does the chorus look like?), dancers can translate choreography into visual sequences (what does the movement feel like as a static image?), playwrights can translate scenes into visual tableaus. Any artist whose work has sensory and emotional dimensions can use AI to translate those dimensions into visual form. The skill is not visual execution; the skill is describing the work's sensory and emotional core with enough specificity that the AI can render it.
