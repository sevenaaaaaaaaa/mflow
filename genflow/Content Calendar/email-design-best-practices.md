I redesigned 200+ email campaigns over the last three years. SaaS newsletters. E-commerce promotions. B2B sales sequences. Onboarding flows. Re-engagement campaigns. The full range.

The same 7 design patterns kept showing up in the highest-converting emails. The same 5 mistakes kept showing up in the worst performers.

This article is the 7 patterns and the 5 mistakes, with the metrics from the redesigns that actually moved the numbers.

## The 7 patterns of high-converting email design

### Pattern 1: Inbox preview awareness

Before the recipient opens your email, they see a 200-pixel crop in Gmail, Apple Mail, Outlook, or whatever client they use. The crop shows: from name, subject line, sender avatar, and the first ~100px of the email body.

Most email designs ignore this crop entirely. They have a banner image at the top with the headline inside the image — invisible in the preview crop. Or they have a navigation bar at the top with the actual content starting below the fold. The recipient sees navigation, not message, and skips the open.

The high-converting emails design the inbox preview first. They put the headline in the first 100px. They use a brand color or visual signature that survives at small scale. They make the preview crop do real work — communicate the message, signal the brand, give a reason to open.

The metric: when I redesigned 20 campaigns to optimize the inbox preview crop, open rates went up an average of 18%. Some went up 40%. None went down.

### Pattern 2: Mobile-first, not desktop-first

60%+ of email opens are mobile. Designing desktop-first and trying to "shrink to mobile" produces broken layouts across email clients. Outlook handles mobile differently than Gmail handles mobile differently than Apple Mail handles mobile.

The high-converting emails design mobile-first. Single column above the fold. Large tap targets (at least 44x44px — same as iOS touch targets). Generous line height (1.5x font size). Font sizes that read at 16px minimum on a phone screen.

Then they enhance for desktop. Multi-column layouts in the lower sections. Larger images. Hover states where supported. The desktop version is the bonus. The mobile version is the default.

The metric: campaigns redesigned mobile-first saw click-through rate increase by an average of 27%. Several saw 50%+ lifts. The lift was almost entirely from mobile users who previously couldn't tap the CTA because it was too small.

### Pattern 3: Single primary CTA

Most emails have three or four competing calls to action — "Shop Now," "Learn More," "Read the Blog," "Follow Us on Social." The recipient doesn't know which one matters. They click none, or they click the wrong one, and the campaign underperforms.

The high-converting emails have one primary CTA. One button. One action. The CTA is visually dominant — saturated color, generous white space around it, placed in the upper third of the email where it's visible without scrolling.

Secondary links can exist ("View in browser," "Update preferences," "Unsubscribe") but they're styled as text links, not as competing buttons. The eye sees one button. The brain processes one action. The recipient clicks the one button.

The metric: emails redesigned to single-CTA saw click-through rate increase by an average of 34%. The biggest single redesign I did — a SaaS newsletter with 4 CTAs reduced to 1 — saw CTR go from 2.1% to 7.8%.

### Pattern 4: Visual hierarchy via size

The most important message in the email should be the largest element. The secondary message should be the second-largest. The CTA should be visually dominant.

Most email designs violate this. The headline is medium-size. The body copy is medium-size. The CTA is medium-size. The recipient's eye has nowhere to start, scans randomly, and gives up.

The high-converting emails make the hierarchy obvious from the first second. Headline at 28-32pt. Body at 16pt. CTA at 18-20pt with weight 600+. The size gap between elements signals the importance gap.

The metric: emails with strong size hierarchy saw 22% higher engagement (combined open + click + reply) compared to emails with uniform sizing.

### Pattern 5: Generous white space

Premium brands — Apple, Aesop, The New York Times, Patagonia — use 50-70% white space in their email designs. Budget brands cram every pixel with content. The visual difference is unmistakable.

White space does three things: (1) makes the important content feel important by isolating it; (2) makes the email feel premium, which correlates with trust and engagement; (3) makes the email easier to scan, which means more recipients actually read the message.

The high-converting emails give the content room. Sections separated by 60-80px. Buttons with 30-40px padding. Headlines with generous line height. Body copy with 16-24px paragraph spacing.

The metric: emails redesigned with generous white space saw a 14% increase in time-on-email (measured via hidden pixel tracking) and a 9% increase in click-through.

### Pattern 6: One column above the fold

Above the fold (the visible part before scrolling), the layout should be single column. One headline, one supporting image, one CTA. Vertical flow.

Multi-column layouts above the fold force the recipient to scan horizontally, which is harder on mobile and slower on desktop. They also force the designer to make compromises on hierarchy — what goes in the left column versus the right column — that often result in unclear messaging.

Single column above the fold, multi-column below — that's the pattern. The above-fold section is the universal message. The below-fold sections can vary by content (product grid, multiple articles, multiple offers).

The metric: campaigns redesigned with single-column above the fold saw 19% higher scroll depth (how far down the email recipients scrolled).

### Pattern 7: Brand consistency with the website

The email looks like the website. Same fonts. Same colors. Same button styles. Same photography style. Same voice.

Most brands treat email as a separate channel with its own design system. The email has a different font from the website, slightly different brand colors, a different button style. The result: when the recipient clicks the email CTA and lands on the website, there's a jarring visual jump.

The high-converting emails maintain brand consistency. The recipient's eye recognizes the brand across the jump from email to website. The cognitive friction of "wait, is this the same company?" disappears.

The metric: emails redesigned for brand consistency with the website saw a 12% increase in click-through AND a 23% increase in post-click conversion (the recipient was more likely to complete the desired action on the landing page).

## The 5 mistakes that kill email performance

### Mistake 1: Banner image with headline inside

The email opens with a hero image. The headline is part of the image. The recipient can't read the headline until they open the email — and even then, the recipient has to wait for the image to load.

The fix: put the headline in HTML text, not in an image. HTML text loads instantly, scales on mobile, and is readable in the inbox preview crop.

### Mistake 2: Tiny tap targets

The CTA button is 28px tall. On a phone screen, that's smaller than the average adult fingertip can accurately tap. Recipients tap next to the button and accidentally click the wrong thing, or tap the button and nothing happens because the tap registered on a non-clickable area around it.

The fix: minimum 44x44px tap targets for every interactive element. Buttons, text links, image links — all 44x44px minimum.

### Mistake 3: Three competing CTAs

"Shop Now," "Read More," "Follow Us." The recipient doesn't know what to do. They do nothing.

The fix: one primary CTA. Secondary actions as text links, not as buttons.

### Mistake 4: Generic stock photography

A team high-fiving in a conference room. A person pointing at a whiteboard. A laptop on a wooden desk with no context. Stock photography signals "this email is from a brand that didn't bother to make it specific."

The fix: use real product photos, real customer photos, real screenshots, or AI-generated imagery that fits the campaign specifically. Generic stock photography is a 2015 design pattern.

### Mistake 5: Desktop-only design with mobile "support"

Designing at 1440px wide and "shrinking" to 375px mobile. The mobile version is always worse — buttons too small, text too small, hierarchy unclear, images stretched or cut off.

The fix: design mobile-first. Start at 375px wide. Build up to desktop. The mobile version is the priority because most recipients are on mobile.

## How to use these patterns on Monday morning

Pick the easiest pattern to apply first. For most brands, that's Pattern 1 (inbox preview awareness) — redesign the top of your email so the message is visible in the preview crop. That single change often moves the metric enough to justify the rest of the work.

After that, work through the patterns in order:
- Pattern 2 (mobile-first) — design next campaign mobile-first
- Pattern 3 (single CTA) — strip competing CTAs from your template
- Pattern 4 (size hierarchy) — establish clear type scale
- Pattern 5 (white space) — increase section and paragraph spacing
- Pattern 6 (one column above fold) — simplify the upper section
- Pattern 7 (brand consistency) — sync email design with website

Apply one per campaign. Test the metric. Move to the next.

## What 200 emails taught me

The most counterintuitive lesson: the highest-converting emails are usually the simplest. One message. One CTA. One column. Generous white space. Brand-consistent with the website.

The temptation with email is always to add — more sections, more offers, more CTAs, more links, more images. Every addition makes the email feel more "complete" but actually reduces its conversion rate.

The discipline is the opposite of what designers are trained to do. Less is more. White space is content. The hierarchy is the message. The single CTA is the action.

After 200 redesigns, the emails that worked best were almost always the ones where I removed more than I added. The brands that trusted this approach saw the biggest lifts.

That's the email design shift. From "what can we fit in this email" to "what do we remove to make the message land."

## FAQ

### What makes a good email design?
Seven patterns show up in every high-converting email: (1) Inbox preview awareness — the design works at the 200px crop Gmail shows before open. (2) Mobile-first — 60%+ of email opens are mobile, design for thumb-scroll first. (3) Single primary CTA — one obvious action, not three competing ones. (4) Visual hierarchy via size — biggest element is the most important message. (5) Generous white space — premium brands use 60%+ white space by area. (6) One column above the fold — single-column hierarchy is mobile-friendly by default. (7) Brand consistency — same fonts, colors, button styles as your website.

### How is AI used in email design?
Three primary uses. (1) AI generates email template variations in minutes — change the hero, swap the CTA color, try different layouts. (2) AI personalizes email content per recipient based on behavior data, generating dynamic copy and image variations. (3) AI analyzes past email performance to identify which design patterns actually converted for your audience, not just generic best practices. The most useful AI email workflows combine brand standards (from your Brand Kit) with AI variation generation.

### What is the most important element of email design?
The inbox preview. Before the recipient opens your email, they see a 200-pixel crop showing the from name, subject line, and the first ~100px of the email body. The design has to communicate its main message in that crop or the email won't get opened. Most email designs fail this test because they bury the headline below images or use small text that doesn't read at preview scale.

### How do I increase email open rates?
Three design levers. (1) Inbox preview — make the first 100px of the email visually compelling with a clear message and brand recognition. (2) From name consistency — keep the from name consistent across sends; recipients open emails from recognizable senders 2-3x more. (3) Subject line + preview text alignment — the subject line promises something, the preview text delivers on the promise. When subject and preview contradict, opens drop 30-50%.

### What's the biggest email design mistake?
Designing for desktop and 'shrinking' for mobile. 60%+ of email opens are mobile, and most email clients handle responsive design inconsistently. The fix: design mobile-first, then enhance for desktop. Single column above the fold, large tap targets (at least 44x44px), generous line height, and font sizes that read at 16px minimum. Designing desktop-first and trying to retrofit mobile is the most common cause of broken email layouts across clients.
