# How Gallery Owner Camille Dubois Curated a 47-Artist AI Exhibition — And Sold Out Before Opening Night

Camille Dubois's gallery, Espace Obscur, was losing relevance.

For 12 years, she had run a respected contemporary art space in the 11th arrondissement — the kind of gallery that critics mentioned in roundups and collectors visited on rainy Saturdays. But by early 2026, the numbers were undeniable: foot traffic down 40% from pre-pandemic, sales concentrated in three established artists, and a mailing list that hadn't grown in 18 months.

"The art world I built my career in was still debating whether digital art was 'real art,'" Camille says. "Meanwhile, a new generation of collectors was buying things they'd never seen in a white cube — digital works, AI collaborations, pieces that existed as much on screens as on walls."

She decided to stake her gallery's future on a proposition the Parisian art establishment considered absurd: a fully AI-generated group exhibition. Forty-seven artists, none of whom had touched a physical medium for these works. Eighty-three pieces, all generated through Lovart.

The show sold out before opening night.

---

## The Background

Espace Obscur had built its reputation on emerging painters and sculptors — the kind of gallery that launched careers. But the economics of physical art were deteriorating. A typical exhibition cost:
- Artist stipends and production: €4,000-8,000
- Framing and installation: €2,000-3,500
- Opening event: €1,500-3,000
- Marketing and PR: €1,000-2,000
- Shipping and insurance: €800-2,000

Total cost per exhibition: €9,300-18,500. Average sales per exhibition: €12,000-20,000. Margin after gallery commission (50%): thin to nonexistent. Three of the last five exhibitions lost money.

"I was running a cultural institution that happened to sell art occasionally," Camille says. "The mission was good. The math was not."

---

## The Pain Points

### 1. The Production Bottleneck

Physical artists produce work slowly — a painter might create 10-15 pieces for a solo show over 6-12 months. This made exhibition planning a year-long process with high uncertainty: an artist could lose momentum, change direction, or produce work that didn't cohere as a show. Camille was gambling six months of gallery overhead on an artist's creative consistency.

### 2. The Price-Point Dead Zone

Emerging artists' work priced at €2,000-5,000 occupied a difficult market position: too expensive for casual buyers, too unproven for serious collectors. The result was slow sales velocity — pieces lingered for months or returned to the artist unsold. The gallery needed work at a price point that encouraged purchase decisions, not deliberation.

### 3. The Relevance Gap

Camille's collector base was aging. Her average buyer was 54. Instagram and TikTok had created a new generation of art-interested audiences — people who spent hours looking at visual work online but had never set foot in a gallery. They collected differently: digital works, prints, accessible editions. Camille's traditional exhibitions had nothing to offer them.

---

## The Turning Point

The idea for the AI exhibition came from an unlikely source: Camille's teenage daughter, who showed her a Lovart-generated image that looked like a contemporary abstract painting. "She asked me, 'Would you show this in the gallery?' I said no automatically. Then I looked at it again and realized I couldn't explain why not."

Camille opened a Lovart account and spent a weekend generating images — abstract compositions, surreal landscapes, textural studies. By Sunday night, she had 200+ images. Roughly 40 of them were genuinely compelling as visual art: compositionally strong, emotionally resonant, visually distinctive.

"The work was speaking to me in a way I didn't expect. It wasn't derivative of existing artists. It wasn't generic 'AI art' with that glossy, over-smoothed look. It had a voice — or rather, 40 different voices, each emerging from different prompt approaches. I realized I wasn't looking at a novelty. I was looking at a medium."

---

## The Solution: Camille's AI Exhibition Model

### Artist Collaborations, Not AI Replacements

Camille didn't generate the work herself. She invited 47 artists — painters, sculptors, photographers, and digital artists from her existing network — to collaborate with Lovart. Each artist received a simple brief: "Use AI to create visual work you couldn't or wouldn't create with your hands. Treat the AI as a collaborator, not a tool."

The results were extraordinary in their diversity:
- A painter known for hyperrealistic portraits generated abstract color-field compositions
- A sculptor generated impossible forms — objects that appeared to defy gravity — as "proposals for future physical works"
- A photographer generated surreal composite landscapes that referenced her documentary work but exceeded its visual language
- Several artists who had never worked digitally produced their most conceptually ambitious work through AI prompting

"The artists who produced the best work were the ones who approached AI with the same creative rigor they brought to their physical practice — iterating, discarding, refining, curating. They didn't 'generate art.' They collaborated with an AI to discover images they recognized as their own."

### The Curation Principle

Camille curated the exhibition around a single theme: "Surfaces and Depths" — work that engaged with texture, materiality, and the tension between digital generation and physical presence. Each piece was printed on archival paper at gallery quality, framed identically in simple white oak, and priced at €400-900 — accessible to first-time buyers and serious collectors alike.

The pricing was deliberate: low enough that purchase was an impulse decision for an art-interested person, high enough to signal quality and seriousness. The average piece price of €566 meant the exhibition could sell out at €47,000 total — a modest sum by gallery standards but highly profitable given the near-zero production cost.

### Digital-Physical Hybrid

The exhibition existed in two forms simultaneously: physical prints on the gallery walls and a virtual viewing room where each piece could be examined in high resolution, including the AI prompt that generated it. This transparency — showing the "making of" alongside the finished work — became a differentiator. Collectors were buying the image and the creative process behind it.

## FAQ

### Is AI-generated art actually art — or just technology pretending?

This question dominates critical discourse but misses the commercial reality: people buy art that moves them, regardless of production method. The 83 buyers of Camille's exhibition weren't buying "AI." They were buying images they wanted to live with, created through a collaboration between an artist's vision and an AI's generative capability. Framing the question as "is it art?" is less useful than asking "does it create value for artists and audiences?" — and the exhibition's sales data answers that question definitively.

### How do you price AI-generated art?

Camille's pricing model: base price of €300 for prints (covering printing, framing, and gallery overhead), plus an artist premium based on the artist's market position and the work's visual complexity. The resulting €400-900 range made pieces accessible to first-time buyers while still providing meaningful revenue to artists (50% commission meant €200-450 per piece to the artist, with zero production cost). This price-point strategy — below the "I need to think about it" threshold — drove the pre-opening sellout.

### Do art collectors take AI-generated work seriously?

The 41 first-time buyers in Camille's exhibition are not traditional "serious collectors" — and that's the point. The exhibition expanded the collector base to include people who had never bought art before. For traditional collectors, the entry point was different: several purchased pieces as "conversation objects" that engaged with the AI-art debate. As AI-generated art develops provenance (exhibition history, critical writing, secondary market activity), collector seriousness will follow — as it did for photography in the 1970s and digital art in the 2010s.

### How do you curate an AI art exhibition — what's the selection criteria?

Camille's criteria: (1) Visual distinctiveness — does the image look like something you haven't seen before? (2) Artistic intention — does the prompt and iteration process reflect a coherent creative decision? (3) Emotional resonance — does the image make you feel something? (4) Technical quality — is the resolution, composition, and print quality gallery-standard? She rejected roughly 70% of submissions, applying the same curatorial rigor she brings to physical exhibitions.

### Can AI-generated art be sold as limited editions?

Yes, and this is a key strategy for value creation. Camille's exhibition offered each work as an edition of 3 plus one artist's proof — standard editioning practice in printmaking and photography. Limiting the edition creates scarcity, which supports pricing and collector confidence. The AI file itself can be deleted after editioning to ensure the edition remains limited — though this requires trust between artist and gallerist.

### What printing specifications produce gallery-quality AI art?

Camille's specs: archival pigment print on Hahnemühle Photo Rag 308gsm (a museum-grade matte paper), printed at 300 DPI minimum, framed in white oak with UV-protective museum glass. Total printing and framing cost per piece: roughly €45-65 depending on size. These specifications meet museum conservation standards and signal quality to collectors. Avoid standard photo-lab prints, which lack the longevity and depth of pigment prints on fine-art paper.

### How do artists maintain their creative identity when using AI?

The artists who produced the strongest work used AI as a medium rather than a shortcut. They developed distinctive prompt vocabularies, iterated extensively (some artists generated 200+ images to find 2-3 worth exhibiting), and applied their existing aesthetic judgment to curation. The AI provided generative capacity; the artist provided vision, taste, and discernment. The result was work that felt continuous with each artist's existing practice while exploring territory their physical work couldn't reach.

## Operator Reframe: What This Case Study Is Really About

Sold-out nights have many causes: collectors, timing, neighborhood, pricing. The controllable craft here is curation ops — artist agreements, disclosure, catalog coherence, and invitation design under a tight budget.

Camille used Lovart as production support for invitations, catalog spreads, and wayfinding — not as a secret ghost artist replacing the 47 names on the walls.

Gold sentence: an AI exhibition fails when the medium is the punchline and wins when the curation standards are as strict as any other show.
