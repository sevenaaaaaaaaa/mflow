# How App Developer Min-Jun Park Generated 1,800 Wallpapers in 10 Days — And His Meditation App Hit #1 in the App Store

[IMAGE 1 PLACEHOLDER — Persona Scenario]

Min-Jun Park's meditation app, *Stillness*, had a visual problem that was strangling its growth.

The app was good — guided meditations with a Korean Zen Buddhist framework, narrated by an actual monk, with careful attention to pacing and silence. The content was unique. The UX was clean. The retention numbers for users who completed their first session were exceptional — 62% Day-7 retention, 41% Day-30, metrics any meditation app would envy.

The problem was the visual experience. *Stillness* offered users a selection of calming background images to accompany their meditation — waves, forests, mountain landscapes, abstract gradients. Min-Jun had licensed 40 stock photos for the launch library. After 6 months, users had seen every image dozens of times. Reviews were starting to mention it: "Love the app but the backgrounds are repetitive." "Wish there were more visual options."

"You're asking someone to close their eyes and meditate. The screen is what they see when they open their eyes. If that screen is boring after the third session, you've failed the visual part of the meditation experience — which is the part that frames the entire practice."

He generated 1,800 AI wallpapers in 10 days. *Stillness* hit #1 in the App Store's Health & Wellness category three weeks later.

## The Background

[IMAGE 2 PLACEHOLDER — Conceptual Diagram]

Min-Jun, 30, is a solo iOS developer in Seoul who built *Stillness* as a side project in 2025. The app gained traction through word of mouth in Korean meditation communities — its unique positioning (Zen Buddhist framework, actual monk narration, Korean-language focus) filled a genuine gap in the meditation-app market.

By early 2026, *Stillness* had 12,000 monthly active users and a 4.7-star rating. The numbers were strong for a solo-developed app. But growth had plateaued, and Min-Jun's user research identified the bottleneck: the visual experience was thin. Forty images. Six months. Users were bored.

The obvious solution — license more stock photography — was expensive and creatively unsatisfying. Quality stock photos cost $10-50 per image for commercial app licensing. A library of 500 images would cost $5,000-25,000 — prohibitive for a solo developer. And stock photos of "calming nature scenes" all looked the same: the same generic waves, the same anonymous forest paths, the same personality-free landscapes that every meditation app used.

---

## The Pain Points

### 1. The Content Depth Problem

Meditation apps require visual depth — a library large enough that users don't encounter repetition within their first 20-30 sessions. With 40 images and users meditating daily, the average user cycled through the entire library in under 2 months. Repetition breaks the meditation experience: the user opens the app, sees an image they've seen before, and the sense of discovery and freshness that supports the practice evaporates.

### 2. The Stock Photo Sameness

The meditation-app visual language has converged on a narrow aesthetic: misty forests, still lakes, mountain peaks at sunrise. Every app uses the same visual vocabulary because every app licenses from the same stock libraries. *Stillness* couldn't differentiate visually using stock photography — it could only look like every other meditation app, which undermined its unique content positioning.

### 3. The Solo-Developer Economics

Min-Jun's monthly revenue was roughly $4,200 — healthy for a side project but insufficient for major content investment. Every dollar spent on stock photography was a dollar not spent on user acquisition, server costs, or feature development. The content depth required for a competitive meditation app was priced for venture-funded competitors (Headspace, Calm) with content budgets in the millions.

## The Solution: How Min-Jun Built the Visual Library

### Thematic Category Design

Min-Jun mapped his meditation library to visual categories, generating content that corresponded to each meditation type:

| Meditation Theme | Visual Category | Images Generated | Prompt Approach |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mountain stillness | Korean mountain landscapes | 350 | Specific mountains, seasons, times of day |
| Ocean awareness | Coastal and underwater scenes | 400 | Korean coastline, varying wave states, tide pools |
| Forest breathing | Forest interiors and details | 350 | Bamboo groves, pine forests, macro botanical |
| Body scan | Abstract flowing forms | 300 | Organic gradients, cellular patterns, energy flows |
| Loving-kindness | Warm, luminous abstract | 250 | Golden light, sunrise/sunset gradients, soft geometry |
| Walking meditation | Paths and journeys | 150 | Forest trails, temple paths, garden walks |

Total: 1,800 images across 6 categories, generated in 10 days.

### The Dynamic Delivery System

Rather than dumping 1,800 images into the app, Min-Jun built a dynamic delivery system:
- **Session-specific matching:** The morning meditation shows dawn imagery; the evening meditation shows sunset and night imagery
- **Progressive discovery:** Users unlock new visual categories as they complete meditation milestones (10 sessions, 30 sessions, 100 sessions)
- **Randomized freshness:** Each session draws from a category pool without repeating images within a 20-session window
- **Seasonal rotation:** Imagery changes with Korean seasons — cherry blossoms in spring, vibrant greenery in summer, golden leaves in autumn, snow scenes in winter

The system transformed the visual experience from "40 static images" to "1,800 dynamically delivered images that feel personally curated to each session."

## FAQ

### Can AI wallpapers be high enough quality for a commercial app?

Yes. Lovart generates at up to 4K resolution — sufficient for all current smartphone displays. Min-Jun exports at 2532x1170 (iPhone 14 Pro resolution) as his standard, which provides sharp rendering on all supported devices. For tablet and desktop versions, 4K export covers those larger displays. Quality is not a resolution issue — it's a creative-direction issue. Well-prompted AI produces commercially excellent imagery.

### How many wallpapers does a meditation or wellness app need?

The minimum viable library is roughly 200-300 images (enough for 6-10 months of daily use without repetition). Min-Jun's 1,800-image library is toward the generous end — appropriate for an app competing on visual experience. The key metric isn't total image count but "sessions before first repeat" — Min-Jun targets 30 sessions (roughly one month of daily use) before any image repeats, which the 1,800-image library achieves comfortably across 6 rotating categories.

### How do I organize AI-generated wallpapers into categories?

Plan categories around your app's content themes — Min-Jun's 6 categories map to his 6 meditation types. Generate in batches per category, using the category name and aesthetic in every prompt to maintain visual coherence within the category. Review each batch for consistency — a forest category should have forest images, not ocean images that drifted in through prompt ambiguity. Curate aggressively; generating 50 images and keeping 35-40 is a typical keeper rate.

### Can AI wallpapers be a premium feature in a freemium app?

Yes, and this is a growing monetization model. Offer a base library of 40-60 images to free users and the full, dynamically delivered library to premium subscribers. Min-Jun's "Daily Wallpaper" premium feature — a new curated wallpaper every morning — is his highest-converting subscription driver. Users who might not pay for "ad-free" or "unlimited sessions" will pay for "a beautiful new image every day."

### Will users know or care that wallpapers are AI-generated?

Min-Jun's experience: users don't ask and don't appear to care. The relevant user feedback is "the new backgrounds are beautiful" — not "are these AI-generated?" Quality and aesthetic resonance matter. Production method doesn't. Min-Jun doesn't explicitly label images as AI-generated within the app (the images are an experiential feature, not a content claim), but he discloses in his app description that "visual content is created using AI image generation technology."

### How do I avoid the "same-looking" problem with AI-generated image libraries?

Vary prompts significantly within each category. For mountain landscapes, vary season, time of day, weather, elevation, foreground elements, color palette, and composition. "Korean mountain at dawn, misty" produces a different image from "Korean mountain at midday, clear sky, autumn colors" — even though both are "mountain landscapes." The key to library diversity is systematic prompt variation: build a prompt matrix (category × season × time of day × weather × composition) and generate across all combinations.

### Can I use AI-generated wallpapers in apps beyond meditation/wellness?

The approach extends to any app with visual backgrounds: weather apps (condition-specific backgrounds — sunny, rainy, snowy), productivity apps (focus-mode backgrounds), reading apps (chapter-transition imagery), fitness apps (workout-type imagery), journaling apps (mood-matched visual prompts), and gaming apps (environment concept art, loading screens, level backgrounds). Any app where users spend time looking at the screen can benefit from a deep, diverse, app-specific visual library.
