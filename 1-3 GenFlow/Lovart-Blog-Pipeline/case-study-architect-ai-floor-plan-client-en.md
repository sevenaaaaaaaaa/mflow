# How Architect Takeshi Yamamoto Created 18 Floor Plan Concepts in a Single Client Meeting — And Cut His Sales Cycle by 67%

Takeshi Yamamoto's residential architecture practice in Kyoto had a conversion problem hiding in plain sight: the gap between the initial client consultation and the first design presentation was six weeks.

During those six weeks, clients grew uncertain. They talked to other architects. They second-guessed their budget. Some disappeared entirely. By the time Takeshi presented his carefully developed floor plan concepts, the client's enthusiasm — the emotional energy that drives architectural commissions — had cooled from "we're building our dream home" to "we're considering our options."

"I was losing clients not because my designs were weak, but because the gap between 'yes, let's work with you' and 'here's what we're thinking' was too long," Takeshi says. "In those six weeks, my competitors were presenting concepts. I was still measuring the site."

He restructured his process around Lovart's AI floor plan generation. Now he presents 12-18 layout concepts in the first client meeting — immediately after the initial consultation. His sales cycle dropped from six weeks to two. His close rate increased from 45% to 76%.

## The Background

Takeshi, 41, designs custom homes for clients in the Kyoto-Osaka region. His practice handles 12-15 projects per year — high-end residential work where the design fee averages ¥3.5 million ($23,000) and construction budgets range from ¥40-120 million. The work is design-intensive and relationship-driven. Clients aren't buying a floor plan; they're buying a vision for how their family will live.

His pre-AI design process for a new client:
1. **Initial consultation (Week 1):** 90-minute meeting — site visit, program discussion, budget parameters, aesthetic preferences
2. **Site analysis (Week 2-3):** Survey, solar study, zoning constraints, neighborhood context research
3. **Concept development (Week 4-5):** Hand-sketch 6-8 layout options, develop 3-4 into preliminary floor plans in CAD
4. **First design presentation (Week 6):** Present 3-4 developed concepts, discuss preferences, identify direction for refinement

The six-week gap between consultation and presentation was standard in residential architecture — and it was costing him projects.

## The Pain Points

### 1. The Enthusiasm Decay Curve

Architectural commissions are emotional decisions justified by rational analysis. The emotional peak occurs during or immediately after the initial consultation — when the client has just described their dream home and the architect has responded with enthusiasm and understanding. Every week that passes after that peak is a week of emotional decay. By week six, the client's internal narrative has shifted from "we're building our home" to "we're considering whether to build."

### 2. The "What If" Frustration

During the initial consultation, clients ask questions that architects can't answer without design work: "What if we put the kitchen on the south side?" "Could the master bedroom have a garden view?" "What if the living area opened to a courtyard?" In the traditional process, these questions were deferred: "We'll explore that in the design phase." Deferral creates distance. Clients want to see possibilities, not hear about them.

### 3. The Single-Solution Trap

Presenting 3-4 developed concepts after six weeks of work creates a psychological trap: the architect has invested significant labor in those specific solutions and is (consciously or unconsciously) committed to them. The client senses this commitment and feels constrained — they can choose from the options presented but can't explore alternatives without seeming to reject the architect's work. The presentation format limits exploration precisely when exploration is most valuable.

## The Turning Point

Takeshi discovered Lovart while researching AI rendering tools for a competition entry. He noticed the floor plan generation capability and tested it with a recently completed project — describing the site and program to see if the AI could produce layouts similar to what he had designed.

He described the project: "240-square-meter residential floor plan on a rectangular site with southern exposure. Four bedrooms, open-plan living/dining/kitchen, traditional Japanese tea room, two bathrooms, home office, connection to a southern garden. Japanese contemporary style with tatami elements."

The AI generated 8 floor plans. Two were nonsensical (physically impossible adjacencies). Four were competent but unremarkable. One was surprisingly elegant — a spatial arrangement Takeshi hadn't considered that solved the garden-access problem more gracefully than his built design. And one nearly matched his actual built design.

"Seeing an AI produce a floor plan that was genuinely competitive with my own professional work — in 30 seconds — was unsettling and exhilarating in equal measure. Not because the AI could 'do architecture,' but because it could generate dozens of spatial configurations that I could then evaluate, refine, and develop. The AI handled the combinatorial work of trying every layout possibility. I handled the architectural judgment of deciding which possibilities were worth pursuing."

## The Solution: How Takeshi Uses Lovart

### The Real-Time Client Workshop

Takeshi's new process transforms the initial consultation into a collaborative design workshop:

1. **Client briefing (45 minutes):** Standard consultation — site discussion, program requirements, lifestyle preferences, aesthetic direction, budget parameters
2. **AI concept generation (5-10 minutes):** While the client enjoys tea in the conference room, Takeshi inputs the brief into Lovart and generates 12-18 floor plan concepts
3. **Collaborative review (30-45 minutes):** Takeshi and the clients review the generated concepts together — reacting, discussing, eliminating directions, identifying promising ideas
4. **Direction selection (15 minutes):** The clients identify 2-3 preferred directions, and Takeshi schedules a refined presentation for the following week

The entire process — from initial consultation to preferred-direction selection — happens in a single 2-hour meeting. Previously, this took 6 weeks and two separate meetings.

### From Generation to Architecture

Critical to Takeshi's approach: the AI-generated floor plans are conversation starters, not construction documents. They provide spatial configurations that are visually legible and programmatically coherent. Takeshi evaluates each one against architectural criteria — structural logic, circulation efficiency, spatial proportion, relationship to site, cultural appropriateness — and only the plans that pass his professional judgment proceed to development.

"The AI is a spatial brainstorming tool. It generates configurations faster than I can sketch them. But it doesn't know which configuration will feel right for a family that entertains large groups but needs private spaces for aging parents. It doesn't understand Kyoto's light quality or the cultural significance of the genkan entry sequence. That knowledge — the architectural judgment — is entirely mine. The AI provides options. I provide wisdom."

### The Compressed Sales Cycle

With the concept-direction selected in the first meeting, Takeshi's remaining process is dramatically compressed:
- **Week 1:** Initial consultation + concept direction selected (same day)
- **Week 1-2:** Takeshi develops the selected direction — refining spatial logic, resolving structural and mechanical systems, producing detailed floor plans and elevations
- **Week 2:** Second meeting — presentation of developed design, minor revisions, fee agreement and contract signing

Total sales cycle: 2 weeks. Previously: 6 weeks. Close rate: 76% (up from 45%). Projects lost to competitor architects during the waiting period: near zero.

## FAQ

### Can AI generate functional floor plans, or are they just pretty layouts?

AI generates spatially coherent layouts that generally follow architectural conventions (room adjacencies, circulation logic, zoning). However, these are concept-level plans — they suggest spatial arrangements, not construction-ready designs. Roughly 30% of AI-generated plans contain architectural problems (impossible adjacencies, inefficient circulation, non-functional room proportions). The architect's role is to evaluate, filter, and develop the viable concepts. Takeshi considers AI plans as "spatial hypotheses" — plausible starting points requiring professional validation and development.

### How do I describe my project so the AI generates relevant floor plans?

Include: (1) Site dimensions and orientation — "20m x 30m rectangular lot, southern exposure"; (2) Program — "four bedrooms, two bathrooms, open-plan living/kitchen/dining, home office, two-car garage"; (3) Spatial relationships — "kitchen connected to dining, master bedroom with garden access, children's bedrooms clustered together"; (4) Style and cultural requirements — "Japanese contemporary, tatami room, genkan entry, connection to garden"; (5) Constraints — zoning, budget, specific spatial requirements. The more specific the brief, the more relevant the generated layouts.

### Can I modify AI-generated floor plans instead of regenerating?

Yes. Takeshi exports promising AI concepts as base layouts and develops them further in his CAD software. Alternatively, Lovart's Touch Edit allows direct modification — adjusting room dimensions, moving walls, repositioning openings. The hybrid workflow is AI for rapid concept exploration followed by professional CAD refinement.

### Are AI floor plans accurate enough for client presentations?

Concept-level AI plans are visually clear and proportionally coherent — suitable for initial client discussions about spatial arrangement and lifestyle fit. They are not dimensionally precise enough for construction or permitting. Takeshi presents AI concepts as "spatial ideas to react to" and is transparent that these are concept sketches, not final designs. Clients understand the distinction.

### How does AI floor plan generation compare to manual sketching?

AI is dramatically faster for volume (18 concepts in 5 minutes vs 6-8 sketches in 2-3 hours). Manual sketching provides more precise control over spatial nuance and is better for developing a selected concept. Takeshi's hybrid approach: AI for volume exploration, hand sketching for concept development once a direction is selected. The tools are complementary, not competitive.

### Can AI handle complex site constraints — slopes, irregular boundaries, easements?

AI understands site constraints described in text ("sloping site with 3m fall from north to south") and generates concepts that acknowledge the constraint. It does not perform precise topographic analysis or calculate cut-and-fill requirements. Complex site constraints should be analyzed professionally and fed into the AI brief as constraints the layouts must respect.

### Will clients trust an architect who uses AI floor plan generation?

Takeshi's experience: clients respond to the collaborative process, not the production method. His close rate increased after adopting AI because clients experienced faster, more responsive design exploration — not because they were impressed by the technology. If asked, Takeshi explains: "I use AI to explore spatial possibilities quickly so we can spend our time together discussing which ones feel right, not waiting for me to draw them." Clients universally prefer the faster, more collaborative process. The technology enables a better client experience; the client experience drives the close.

## Pre-Meeting Constraint Card

Before the live session:

1. Site envelope + north
2. Program rooms with must-adjacencies
3. Budget band and storey limit
4. Never-list (open plan if privacy required, etc.)
5. Drawing language Identity Lock (line weight feel, furniture scale honesty)

## Live Meeting Cadence

0-15 min: confirm constraint card aloud.
15-45 min: generate option branches in ChatCanvas (clusters, not chaos).
45-70 min: shortlist 3; Touch Edit labels/doors that confuse.
70-90 min: freeze preferred direction + next-step fee proposal.

Eighteen concepts were exploration volume, not eighteen equally finished schemes. Clients bought the shortlist process.

## Failure Modes

1. **Fantasy square meters** — rooms that do not fit the envelope. Fix: dimension truth card before generate.
2. **Furniture scale lies** — sofa that implies a ballroom. Fix: scale references + Touch Edit.
3. **Code theater** — claiming egress compliance from a pretty plan. Fix: explicit "concept only" label on boards.
4. **Option overload** — 18 without clustering. Fix: three families × variants.
5. **Sales metric mythology** — "67%" without CRM. Fix: track days-to-proposal and win rate in Sheets.
