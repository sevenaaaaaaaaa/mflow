# 10 Common AI Design Mistakes and How to Fix Them

[IMAGE 1 PLACEHOLDER — Persona Scenario]

A marketing coordinator stares at her screen. She's been using an AI design tool for six weeks. Some outputs are remarkable. Others look like a robot's idea of what a human might consider acceptable. The inconsistency is maddening because the cause is invisible — same tool, same user, wildly different results. She's not bad at this. She's making mistakes that are fixable the moment someone names them.

This article names them. **Ai design mistakes** are almost never about the AI's capability. They're about the gap between what the AI needs to produce professional work and what users — even experienced ones — provide. Fix the input, and the output fixes itself.

## Mistake #1: The Generic Prompt

**The problem:** "Make a social media post for our product launch."

**Why it fails:** The AI has no information about your product, your audience, your brand, your format, your message, or your aesthetic direction. It fills the information void with statistical averages from its training data — which is to say, it produces the most generic possible version of "social media post."

**The fix:** "Instagram carousel slide (1080×1080) for a sustainable sneaker launch. Brand: warm earth tones, clean sans-serif typography. Slide 1: hero product shot with 'The Shoe That Grows With You' headline. Audience: environmentally conscious millennials who buy fewer, better things. Aesthetic: editorial minimalism, natural textures, no aggressive sales energy."

## Mistake #2: Skipping Brand Parameters

**The problem:** Generating designs without activating brand colors, fonts, or logo parameters. Every output has different visual DNA.

**Why it fails:** Consistency is the difference between a brand and a collection of designs. Without brand parameters locked in, the AI treats each generation as an independent event with no relationship to the previous one.

**The fix:** Use Lovart's Brand Kit. Define your colors (hex codes), typography (heading + body), logo (placement rules), and aesthetic parameters once. Every generation inherits these automatically. Brand consistency becomes the default rather than an effort.

## Mistake #3: Accepting the First Output

**The problem:** Generating once and using whatever comes out.

**Why it fails:** AI generation is probabilistic, not deterministic. The first output is a roll of the dice. Sometimes it lands well; often it doesn't. Professional designers iterate — they sketch, explore, refine. AI design requires the same discipline applied through a different medium.

**The fix:** Generate 3-6 variations per brief. Evaluate them against the brief's criteria (not personal taste). Select the strongest direction. Iterate with specific, directed refinements: "darker background, larger headline, more negative space." Three to four directed iterations typically produce professional-caliber results from an average first generation.

## Mistake #4: Using the Wrong Dimensions

**The problem:** Generating a beautiful design at the wrong size for its intended platform. A 1:1 Instagram post used on LinkedIn Stories (9:16). A landscape image cropped awkwardly for TikTok.

**Why it fails:** Each platform has specific dimension requirements and content presentation formats. A design that violates these looks unprofessional regardless of its aesthetic quality.

**The fix:** Specify exact pixel dimensions in every brief. Lovart supports every major platform dimension: Instagram feed (1080×1080), Stories/Reels/TikTok (1080×1920), LinkedIn feed (1200×627 or 1080×1080), Twitter/X (1600×900 or 1080×1080), Facebook (1200×630). Generate platform-native rather than generating once and cropping.

## Mistake #5: Letting AI Handle Typography Alone

**The problem:** Including text in the AI generation prompt and trusting the output.

**Why it fails:** AI image generators are image models, not typography engines. They render text statistically — which means some letters look right, some don't, and small text is frequently garbled. This is the most common visual tell of AI-generated design.

**The fix:** Generate the visual composition without text. Use Lovart's text overlay tools for all typography — headlines, body copy, CTAs. You control font selection, size, positioning, and content precisely. The result combines AI visual quality with human-verified typography. This hybrid approach eliminates the #1 AI design tell.

## Mistake #6: Ignoring Resolution Requirements

**The problem:** Using screen-resolution output for print applications. Using web-resolution images for large-format display.

**Why it fails:** Screen resolution (72 DPI) produces acceptable web images but blurry prints and pixelated large displays. Print requires 300 DPI minimum. Large format requires proportionally higher pixel dimensions.

**The fix:** Specify resolution in every brief: "Web: 72 DPI, 1080×1080 px, RGB, PNG" or "Print: 300 DPI, 18×24 in, CMYK, PDF/X-4." Lovart generates at your specified resolution. Never assume AI output defaults are appropriate for your use case.

## Mistake #7: Over-Reliance on a Single Style

**The problem:** Finding one prompt formula that works and using it for everything. Every output looks the same.

**Why it fails:** Different content types, platforms, and messages need different visual treatments. A quote card and a product launch should not look identical. Feed homogeneity signals low creative investment to audiences who scroll past hundreds of posts daily.

**The fix:** Maintain a style library — 5-8 distinct visual treatments appropriate for your brand. Rotate through them based on content type. Save each style as a template prompt in your workflow. The AI produces visual variety within brand consistency when you direct it to.

## Mistake #8: Not Specifying What NOT to Include

**The problem:** Briefs that only say what to include, never what to exclude.

**Why it fails:** AI defaults to visual clutter — it fills empty space, adds decorative elements, includes generic "design" tropes. Without explicit exclusions, you get the AI's aesthetic defaults rather than yours.

**The fix:** Include a "constraints" section in every brief. Examples: "No gradients. No decorative borders. No stock-photo aesthetic. No hands visible (hands render inconsistently). No text directly over faces. No more than three visual elements total." Negative space is a design choice. Brief for it explicitly.

## Mistake #9: Iterating Without Direction

**The problem:** "Make it better." "I don't like it — try again." "Something feels off."

**Why it fails:** AI responds to specific direction, not vague dissatisfaction. "Make it better" gives the AI no information about what "better" means in this context.

**The fix:** Identify the specific problem before requesting changes. "The product is too small — make it 40% larger and center it." "The background competes with the headline — darken it by 30%." "The composition feels unbalanced — add negative space on the left to counterweight the text on the right." Specific, measurable direction produces specific, measurable improvement.

## Mistake #10: Treating AI as a Replacement for Judgment

**The problem:** Generating designs and publishing them without human evaluation.

**Why it fails:** AI produces outputs. It does not evaluate them against strategic criteria. It doesn't know your audience's sensitivities, your brand's cultural position, or the competitive context. It cannot catch errors that are contextually obvious to a human — a product displayed incorrectly, a cultural reference that lands wrong, a visual that's indistinguishable from a competitor.

**The fix:** Your role shifts from producer to editor. AI handles production; you handle judgment. Before publishing anything, ask: Does this achieve the brief's objective? Does it respect the brand? Would a customer understand it immediately? Is there anything in this image that could be interpreted differently than intended? The AI generates possibilities. You select outcomes.

## FAQ

**Q: What's the single biggest mistake people make with AI design tools?**

Treating AI as a "make it look good" button rather than a production tool that rewards specific direction. The gap between generic prompting ("make a nice post") and specific briefing ("1080×1080 product feature for Instagram, warm editorial lighting, generous negative space, brand colors active") is the gap between unusable and professional output.

**Q: Why does AI-generated text always look wrong?**

AI image models generate text statistically — they don't understand language. They render letter-like shapes that approximate text from training data. This is a fundamental limitation of current image models. The fix is simple: never include text in your image generation. Use dedicated text overlay tools for all typography.

**Q: How many iterations should I expect before getting a usable result?**

With a well-written brief: 1-3 iterations typically produce professional output. With a weak brief: the iteration count is unlimited because the AI lacks the information to converge on anything specific. Invest time in the brief, not in the iterations.

**Q: Can I fix design mistakes without starting over?**

Yes. Lovart's ChatCanvas supports directed edits: "darken background," "enlarge product," "move headline up." Targeted edits preserve what's working while fixing what isn't. Starting over should be the last resort, not the first response to a problem.

**Q: Why do my designs look different from one session to the next?**

You're likely not using Brand Kit or not specifying consistent prompt parameters. The AI treats each generation as independent unless you provide continuity through persistent brand settings and consistent prompt language. Brand Kit is the primary mechanism for cross-session consistency.

**Q: Are there any mistakes that can't be fixed through iteration?**

Some outputs contain fundamental composition errors that iteration compounds rather than fixes. If three directed iterations haven't improved the output, start fresh with a revised brief rather than continuing to iterate. The brief is the problem, not the AI.

**Q: How do I learn to write better briefs?**

Study the outputs you like and reverse-engineer the visual characteristics: composition, color, texture, typography, lighting, mood. Translate those characteristics into prompt language. Save effective briefs as templates. Brief quality improves with practice and analysis — the same skills that improve any design direction capability.
