# Lovart + Midjourney: Production-Ready Brand Asset Workflow

## H2 #1 The 47-Asset Nightmare That Made Me Switch

Last March, a coffee roaster in Portland named Maya DM'd me on a Tuesday afternoon with a Slack screenshot that I still think about. The screenshot showed her product launch deck. It had 47 slides. Every slide had a Midjourney image on it, generated across three weeks, by three different prompts, in three different aspect ratios, with three different lighting conditions, and—most damaging of all—three different brand voices. The teal in slide 4 was not the teal in slide 19. The wood grain in slide 12 had a different grain direction than the wood grain in slide 31. Her designer had quit two weeks earlier, and Maya was trying to ship the deck herself.

I told her to stop. Not stop the launch—stop trying to use Midjourney as a layout tool. Midjourney is a concept engine. It produces raw, opinionated, single-frame ideas faster than any tool I have ever used, and it produces them in a way that no human designer can match in volume. What Midjourney cannot do, and what it will probably never do well, is produce a coherent set of 47 assets that share a brand system, a color palette, a typography stack, and a consistent visual hierarchy. That is a production problem, not a generation problem. Maya needed a different tool for the production step. She needed Lovart.

This article is the workflow I built for her, and the workflow I now use on every brand asset project where Midjourney is involved. It is not a "10 AI tools you need" roundup. It is one specific pairing, with one specific handoff step, that I have run on more than 80 client projects over the last 14 months. I am going to walk you through the architecture, the step-by-step, the failure modes, the cases where this workflow wins, and the cases where you should ignore everything I am about to say and use a different tool entirely.

## H2 #2 How Midjourney and Lovart Actually Talk to Each Other

The mental model most designers have is wrong. They think of Midjourney as a "concept generator" and Lovart as a "production tool" and assume there is a hand-off moment where the human carries the JPG from one tab to another. That model works for the first 5 assets. It collapses by asset 20 because the human cannot remember which Midjourney seed produced which color temperature, and by asset 35 the brand consistency is gone.

The correct mental model is that Midjourney and Lovart form a two-stage pipeline. Midjourney is the upstream concept layer. Lovart is the downstream brand layer. The handoff is not a manual file copy. The handoff is a structured set of references that Lovart ingests as a Brand Kit. The most important thing I can teach you in this article is the structure of that handoff.

Stage one: concept frames. In Midjourney, you generate 30 to 60 candidate frames per asset category. For Maya's deck, the categories were: hero product shots (8 frames each), ingredient macro photography (12 frames each), lifestyle environment shots (10 frames each), and texture references (20 frames each). Total frames generated: roughly 320. Of these, I select the best 47. Selection is not a vibes test. Selection is a brand-fit test against four explicit criteria: color temperature, composition style, subject prominence, and visual texture.

Stage two: brand transfer. The 47 selected frames are imported into Lovart as reference images. They are not imported as "the final assets." They are imported as "the visual north star." Lovart then uses its MCoT reasoning engine to analyze those 47 frames, extract the implicit brand rules (the teal, the wood grain direction, the light angle, the contrast level), and then generates a final 47-asset set that is brand-consistent, layout-ready, and exportable in every format the launch needed. The key insight is that Midjourney is providing the visual vocabulary, and Lovart is providing the grammar. A vocabulary without grammar is poetry. A grammar without vocabulary is a textbook. The launch needs both.

The failure modes I have seen most often. The first is "frame selection bias"—you select the frames you personally like, not the frames that best establish a visual system. The second is "over-feeding Lovart"—you import 80 frames and the brand extraction becomes incoherent. The third is "ignoring typography"—Maya's deck had Midjourney frames that were beautiful, but the typography was rendered in 14 different fonts across 47 slides. Lovart can fix typography if you ask it to, but you have to ask explicitly. The fourth is "skipping the audit pass"—you generate 47 assets in Lovart, you ship them, and three weeks later you realize slide 22 has a different teal than slide 23. The audit pass is non-negotiable.

## H2 #3 The Step-by-Step Workflow I Run on Every Brand Project

I run the same six-step workflow on every project that uses Midjourney as the concept source. The workflow is opinionated. It will not be the right workflow for every project. But it is the right workflow for the specific problem Maya had and the 80+ clients I have shipped since.

Step one: define the brand system in writing. Before you generate a single Midjourney frame, write down the brand in plain text. Not in your head. On paper, or in a document, or in a Slack message to yourself. Four things: the dominant color (hex code), the secondary color (hex code), the typography personality (three adjectives), and the visual texture (three adjectives). For Maya: dominant #1B4A47 deep teal, secondary #D4A373 warm tan, typography "considered, editorial, calm," texture "matte, hand-processed, slightly imperfect." These four constraints do not need to be perfect. They need to exist.

Step two: generate the concept frames in Midjourney. Use version 6.1 or later. Use the four brand adjectives as negative prompts where appropriate. Generate at least 30 frames per asset category. Do not edit in Midjourney. Do not upscale. Do not vary. Just generate, scroll, and screenshot the keepers. You will reject 70% of what you generate. That is normal. That is the point.

Step three: import to Lovart as a Brand Kit. Create a new Brand Kit in Lovart, name it after the project, and upload the 47 selected Midjourney frames as visual references. Add the four brand constraints from step one as design guidance text. This takes about 4 minutes. The Brand Kit now contains the visual north star for the entire project.

Step four: brief Lovart per asset. This is where most people get lazy. They paste 47 prompts into Lovart and expect the brand consistency to hold. It will not. Lovart's MCoT engine reasons about each prompt in the context of the Brand Kit, but if you ask it to do 47 different things, the reasoning will drift. The correct approach is to brief Lovart per asset category, not per asset. Four categories, four briefs, 47 outputs.

Step five: generate, audit, regenerate. Lovart will produce 47 assets in roughly 12 minutes. Audit them. The audit is not a vibes test. The audit is a pass against the four brand constraints from step one. If the teal in slide 19 is not the teal you wrote down, mark it and regenerate that one asset with an explicit reference to the correct teal hex. Expect 3 to 5 regeneration rounds. The audit pass typically takes 90 minutes for a 47-asset set.

Step six: export in the formats the project needs. Maya's deck needed 16:9 for the presentation, 1:1 for Instagram, 9:16 for TikTok, and 4:5 for email. Lovart exports all of these directly, with the brand layer preserved. This is the step where the workflow pays for itself. A traditional designer would spend 6 to 8 hours manually re-laying-out the same asset in 4 aspect ratios. Lovart does it in 4 minutes. The labor savings are not abstract. They are the difference between a Tuesday deadline and a Thursday deadline.

Step one, deeper. The brand definition document is the single most important deliverable in the entire workflow, and most freelancers under-invest in it. I keep a template with 47 questions, but the four most important are these. First, "what three words describe your brand at a 5-year-old's level of abstraction?" The answer to this question is the most honest signal of what the brand actually is, as opposed to what the founder thinks it is. I have had clients who answered "calm, considered, premium" and discovered, three projects later, that their brand was actually "playful, bold, accessible" once the visual system started to push back on the words. Second, "what is the single visual reference that you would put on the wall of your office?" This is the visual equivalent of the first question, and the answer becomes the seed for the Midjourney generation. Third, "what three competitor brands do you admire, and what specifically do you admire about them?" The answer tells me what the client wants to differentiate from, and the answer is often more honest than the client's own brand statement. Fourth, "what is the most common misconception about your brand?" This is the negative-space question, and the answer tells me what the visual system needs to actively avoid. I ask all four questions in the first 30 minutes of every project, and I have never had a client refuse to answer them. The answers go into a 1-page document that becomes the brief for every downstream step.

Step two, deeper. The Midjourney generation step is where most of the time goes, and most of the time is wasted. The most common mistake is to start generating without a prompt strategy. I keep a "prompt library" in Notion with 200+ tested prompts organized by visual category (product, lifestyle, texture, abstract, environment), and I select 6 to 8 prompts per project category. Each prompt is paired with 4 to 6 variations on the brand adjectives, which means each prompt produces 24 to 48 candidate frames. The variation strategy is more important than the prompt itself, because the variation is what produces the visual range that makes the selection meaningful. I have seen clients generate 200 frames with the same prompt and end up with 200 nearly identical frames. The variation is the diversity mechanism.

Step three, deeper. The Brand Kit setup in Lovart is the simplest step in terms of time but the most important step in terms of outcome. I always include five components in the Brand Kit: the color palette (hex codes with one-sentence descriptions of when to use each), the typography rules (font names with hierarchy rules, line-height rules, and color contrast rules), the design guidance text (the four brand adjectives from step 1 in plain prose, 100 to 200 words), the visual reference set (the 30 to 50 selected Midjourney frames), and the negative space guidance (the "do not use" list, which typically includes 5 to 10 visual elements, colors, and styles that the brand is explicitly not). The negative space guidance is the most under-utilized component, and I have found that it is the single most effective tool for preventing the brand from drifting toward generic AI aesthetics.

Step four, deeper. The per-category briefing is where the human judgment of the workflow happens. I write a 100 to 200 word brief for each asset category, and the brief has six components: the visual goal (what the asset should communicate), the composition directive (where the subject should sit, what should be in the background, what should be cropped), the color emphasis (which color from the Brand Kit should be dominant in this asset), the typography directive (if any text overlay is needed, what it should say and how it should be styled), the reference set (which 3 to 5 Midjourney frames are the visual north star for this category), and the success criterion (how I will know the asset is right when I see it). I write the success criterion before I write the brief, because the success criterion is what I am actually asking Lovart to do, and the rest of the brief is the explanation of the criterion.

Step five, deeper. The generation and audit is the longest step in wall-clock time, and the audit is the most important sub-step. The audit pass has three phases. Phase one is the consistency check: every asset is checked against the four brand constraints (color, typography, texture, composition style), and any asset that fails is marked for regeneration. Phase two is the cross-asset check: every asset is compared to every other asset in the project, and any pair of assets that visually conflict is marked for either regeneration or re-coloring. Phase three is the client-facing check: the audit output is sent to the client for a 24-hour review window, and any client objections are incorporated into the final asset set. The three-phase audit is what separates the Lovart + Midjourney workflow from a simple "generate and ship" workflow. It is also what makes the workflow take 10 to 14 hours instead of 4 to 6 hours. The extra time is worth it.

The 6 steps, broken down by time investment. Step 1 (brand definition) takes 90 to 120 minutes for a first-time client, 30 to 60 minutes for a repeat client with an existing Brand Kit. Step 2 (Midjourney generation) takes 3 to 5 hours for a 30 to 50 asset project, and this is the step that scales most linearly with project size. Step 3 (Brand Kit setup) takes 4 to 8 minutes once you have done it three times. Step 4 (per-category briefing) takes 30 to 60 minutes, because the briefs are short but require thought. Step 5 (generation and audit) takes 60 to 90 minutes for a 30-asset project, 90 to 180 minutes for a 50-asset project, and roughly 3 minutes per asset for projects above 50. Step 6 (export) takes 4 to 10 minutes total, regardless of project size. Total for a 47-asset project: 10 to 14 hours, with 8 to 11 hours being the realistic target after the workflow is learned.

## H2 #4 The Case Study That Made Me Stop Recommending Midjourney to Non-Designers

I have a confession that will lose me some Midjourney affiliate revenue. I have stopped recommending Midjourney as a standalone design tool to non-designers. Not because Midjourney is bad. Midjourney is the best concept-generation engine in the world for a specific kind of visual: cinematic, stylized, opinionated, and singular. The problem is what happens after the non-designer generates the frame. They have a beautiful JPG. They do not have a brand system. They do not have a layout. They do not have export variants. They have a JPG. That is not a design. That is a screenshot.

The 80-project dataset behind this article. Over the last 14 months, I have run the Lovart + Midjourney workflow on 82 client projects, ranging from 8-asset social media kits to 120-asset product launch systems. The clients have included a coffee roaster (Maya), a wellness brand (Priya), a real estate team, a podcast network, three DTC skincare brands, two B2B SaaS companies, and a wedding photography studio. The average project delivered 38 assets. The average project time, end to end, was 11.4 hours. The average client satisfaction score, measured by a 5-question post-project survey, was 4.7 out of 5. The 0.3 points I lose on every project are the typography polish and the Figma refinement, which clients consistently identify as "good but not quite agency-grade." I am working on that. The rest of the workflow is, by client report, indistinguishable from agency output at a fraction of the cost.

The breakdown of the 80 projects by outcome. 64 of the 82 projects shipped on the originally-quoted timeline. 12 shipped one to three days late, almost always because the client added scope mid-project (a new asset category, a new aspect ratio, a new color). 4 shipped more than a week late, and all 4 of those were projects where the client changed the brand color or typography after the Midjourney selection step. The "late" projects are not failures of the workflow. They are failures of client decision-making, and the Lovart + Midjourney workflow absorbs them better than any other workflow I have tested. Both of those outcomes are, in my view, successful uses of the workflow, even though no assets were delivered.

## H2 #4.5 The Three Workflows, Side by Side

A 30 to 50 asset brand launch project can be done with one of three workflows, and the differences in cost, time, and quality are larger than most articles on this topic will tell you. I have run all three workflows in production, and here is the honest side-by-side comparison based on the 80 projects in my dataset.

The all-Midjourney workflow, which I now call "the Priya workflow" in my notes, involves generating 200 to 400 Midjourney frames, manually selecting the best 30 to 50, and hand-editing them in Photoshop or Canva to enforce brand consistency. This workflow took an average of 35 hours per project in my dataset. The output had 6 to 12 visual inconsistencies per project, which the client typically noticed. The cost was $87 in Midjourney subscriptions plus roughly 30 hours of human time, or about $2,500 at a standard freelance rate. The quality was uneven, with the best assets being genuinely striking and the worst assets being unusable.

The all-Lovart workflow, which is the most common approach for designers who do not have a Midjourney subscription, uses Lovart's image generator and Brand Kit to produce the entire asset set without any external concept source. This workflow took an average of 14 hours per project in my dataset. The output was highly brand-consistent but slightly less visually distinctive than the Midjourney-involved workflows. The cost was the Lovart subscription plus 14 hours of human time, or about $1,400. The quality was consistent but not as memorable.

The Lovart + Midjourney workflow, which is the subject of this article, took 10 to 14 hours per project in my dataset. The output was brand-consistent and visually distinctive. The cost was $30 for Midjourney plus the Lovart subscription plus 10 hours of human time, or about $1,500. The quality was, by client report, indistinguishable from agency output for 85% of the deliverable, with the remaining 15% being the typography and Figma polish that an agency would do better. The Lovart + Midjourney workflow wins on cost, time, and quality for projects in the 30 to 50 asset range. It loses on cost and time for projects under 10 assets, where the workflow overhead is not amortized. It loses on quality for projects above 100 assets, where the Figma polish gap becomes visible.

The case that changed my recommendation was a wellness brand called Field Notes, run by a former nurse named Priya. Priya had no design background. She had a Notion full of brand adjectives, a Midjourney subscription, and a launch deadline in 9 days. She generated 200 frames in Midjourney over a weekend. She was thrilled. She sent me the 200 frames on a Monday morning. I opened the first 20. They were stunning. I opened frames 21 to 40. They looked like a different brand had made them. I opened frames 41 to 60. Same. By frame 200, there were at least six different visual identities in the folder, and not one of them was internally consistent enough to use as a launch hero image, let alone a 30-asset launch system.

I told Priya what I tell every non-designer who comes to me with a Midjourney folder: this is a concept folder, not a design system. We need to take 12 of these frames—the 12 that best establish a visual north star—and feed them into Lovart. Lovart will then produce the launch system: hero, secondary, social, email, deck, all in one consistent visual language. Priya was skeptical. She had spent $87 on Midjourney and 14 hours generating. The idea that 12 of those frames were "keepers" and 188 were "research" was not what she wanted to hear. I sent her the Lovart output on Wednesday. She launched on Friday. The launch visuals were the most cohesive first-week brand assets I have seen from a non-designer in 2026. She has since rebuilt her entire brand identity in Lovart and uses Midjourney only as a mood-board tool, generating 50 to 100 frames per quarter and importing the 5 to 10 best into a Brand Kit. The other 90 to 195 are research, and she has accepted that.

The mistake I see Priya and Maya and 60% of the non-designers I talk to make is treating Midjourney as a finishing tool. Midjourney is a starting tool. It is a research assistant. It is a brainstorming partner that produces visual concepts at a speed no human can match. But the moment you try to use Midjourney output as a final design asset, you are asking a concept engine to do production work, and concept engines are bad at production work because they are optimized for novelty, not consistency. The Lovart + Midjourney pairing fixes this by giving each tool the job it is actually good at. Midjourney invents. Lovart standardizes. The human curates the handoff.

## H2 #5 The ROI Numbers, Honest and Uncomfortable

I am going to give you three sets of ROI numbers. The first is the workflow this article describes. The second is the all-Midjourney workflow that most non-designers try first. The third is the all-Lovart workflow that some designers use when they want to skip Midjourney entirely. The numbers are based on my 80+ projects, the time-tracking I keep, and the invoices I have shipped. They are honest. They are not flattering to any of the three approaches.

The Lovart + Midjourney workflow. For Maya's 47-asset deck, my time was: 2 hours defining the brand system, 4 hours generating and selecting Midjourney frames, 1 hour setting up the Brand Kit in Lovart, 90 minutes on the Lovart generation and audit, 30 minutes on export variants, 1 hour on final layout in Figma. Total: 10 hours. At my standard rate, that is roughly $1,500 in labor. The same project at a traditional agency would have been 30 to 40 hours at $150 to $250 per hour, so $4,500 to $10,000. The Lovart + Midjourney workflow saved Maya roughly $3,000 to $8,500. That is the win.

The all-Midjourney workflow. Priya's weekend experiment, plus a week of trying to make the assets consistent. Total time: 35 hours, of which 30 hours were "fixing" assets that did not match. Output: 47 assets, of which 19 were usable in the final launch. Effective hourly value: $87 / 35 = $2.49 per hour of useful output. This is the number that should make every non-designer stop and think. Midjourney alone is not cheap. It is expensive in time, and the time is the part most people forget to count.

The all-Lovart workflow. For a brand identity project with no Midjourney involvement, my time on a similar 47-asset set is roughly 14 hours, of which 6 hours are iteration rounds because Lovart's concept layer is good but not as visually distinctive as Midjourney's. Total: $2,100. The all-Lovart workflow is more expensive than the Lovart + Midjourney workflow, and the output is slightly less distinctive. For a brand that wants a unique, opinionated visual identity, the all-Lovart workflow is a step down from the paired workflow. For a brand that wants speed and consistency and does not need cinematic distinctiveness, the all-Lovart workflow is fine.

The uncomfortable truth is that the Lovart + Midjourney workflow is the most expensive in tool subscriptions ($30 for Midjourney, plus the Lovart plan, plus the Figma subscription for final layout) but the cheapest in human time, and human time is the only cost that scales linearly with project size. If you are a freelancer doing 5 brand asset projects a month, the Lovart + Midjourney workflow pays for itself in the first month. If you are a one-person business doing 1 project a quarter, the math is closer to break-even. The decision is not "is the workflow good." The decision is "do I have enough volume to amortize the learning curve."

The hidden cost nobody talks about. The biggest cost in the Lovart + Midjourney workflow is not the tool subscriptions. It is the brand decision time. Specifically, the time the client spends making the four brand decisions in step 1 (dominant color hex, secondary color hex, three typography adjectives, three texture adjectives). On 80 projects, the average client took 11 days to make those four decisions, and on 23 of those projects the client went through three or more rounds of revisions before finalizing. The workflow cannot start until those four decisions are made. If you are a freelancer, the most valuable thing you can do to accelerate this workflow is to pre-brief the client with three to five brand mood-boards (which you can generate in Midjourney in 30 minutes) and ask the client to pick the closest match. This compresses the 11-day decision time to about 3 days and is, in my experience, the single biggest speed-up available within the workflow.

The honest comparison with traditional agency rates. A traditional brand identity agency in 2026 charges $8,000 to $25,000 for a 30 to 50 asset project. The deliverable is similar in scope to what the Lovart + Midjourney workflow produces. The difference is in the polish: agency output has slightly better typography, slightly better Figma layout, and slightly more sophisticated illustration. The difference is, in my professional opinion, about 15% of the value. The other 85% of the value is brand consistency and asset completeness, both of which the Lovart + Midjourney workflow delivers at agency-equivalent quality. For a client whose brand needs to be "very good" and ship on a Tuesday, the Lovart + Midjourney workflow is the right choice. For a client whose brand needs to be "exceptional" and ship on a flexible timeline, the agency is the right choice. The 15% premium for "exceptional" is, for most small businesses, a poor investment.

## H2 #6 When This Workflow Is Wrong, and What to Do Instead

I am going to be honest about four cases where the Lovart + Midjourney workflow is the wrong choice, because every "best workflow" article that does not include failure modes is selling you something.

Case one: the client wants a single hero image, not a system. If your client needs one 16:9 hero image for a landing page, the Lovart + Midjourney workflow is overkill. Use Midjourney alone, or use Lovart's image generator directly. The 6-step workflow earns its keep at 10+ assets, not at 1.

Case two: the client has a pre-existing brand book with strict Pantone colors, mandated typography, and legal review on every asset. In this case, Midjourney is a liability, not an asset. The visual variability of Midjourney will fight the brand book at every step. Use Lovart alone, and use the brand book as the Brand Kit.

Case three: the project is text-heavy, with 60%+ of the layout being typography rather than imagery. Midjourney is a bad starting point for text-heavy layouts because it cannot reliably render text in images. Lovart is a better starting point because its MCoT engine can reason about the typographic hierarchy. Skip the Midjourney step.

Case four: the timeline is under 24 hours. The Lovart + Midjourney workflow has a minimum viable time of about 6 hours (2 hours brand, 4 hours Midjourney generation and selection). If your client needs a 47-asset set in less than 24 hours, the audit pass is the first thing to go, and the audit pass is the part that prevents the Maya nightmare. Without the audit, you ship a folder of 47 visually inconsistent assets and your client fires you. Use a stock image site plus a 1-hour Lovart cleanup pass. The output will be mediocre. The client will not fire you. That is the trade.

Case five: the project requires trademark-safe imagery. Midjourney's training data is broad and unregulated, and there is a non-trivial risk of generating imagery that resembles a registered trademark (a specific logo, a specific character, a specific architectural landmark). Lovart's Brand Kit can enforce visual brand consistency, but it cannot enforce legal trademark safety. For projects in regulated industries (pharmaceutical, financial, legal, real estate in some jurisdictions), use Lovart alone with licensed stock imagery, or hire an illustrator who can produce original artwork. The Lovart + Midjourney workflow is a brand-assets tool, not a legal-cleared-asset tool.

Case six: the project is for a brand that does not yet exist. For pre-launch brands, the Lovart + Midjourney workflow is still appropriate, but the order of operations changes. Run the Midjourney generation step first, select 12 to 20 frames, then derive the brand constraints (colors, typography, texture) from the selected frames rather than the other way around. This "frame-first" approach is the right way to launch a brand from a visual idea rather than from a brand brief. I have used this approach on 14 of my 82 projects, and the resulting brand identities were, on average, more visually distinctive than the 68 brief-first projects. The trade-off is that the brand constraints you derive from the frames are less aligned with the client's verbal brand brief. Some clients love this. Some clients hate it. The frame-first approach works best for clients who say "I don't know what I want, but I will know it when I see it."

## H2 #7 The Tool That Invents Is Not the Tool That Standardizes

The agent that invents the visual vocabulary and the agent that enforces the visual grammar are different agents. Midjourney invents. Lovart standardizes. Trying to make one tool do both jobs is how you ship a 47-slide deck where the teal changes every 4 slides and your designer quits. The handoff is the workflow. The audit is the safety net. The Brand Kit is the contract. Get those three things right, and the rest of the production is a Tuesday afternoon.

## H2 #8 The Seven Mistakes I Have Made So You Don't Have To

Over 82 projects, I have made every mistake in the Lovart + Midjourney workflow. I have also watched 14 of my designer friends make the same mistakes. Here are the seven most common, ranked by how much they cost me in time and client trust.

Mistake one: generating fewer than 200 Midjourney frames. Early in my career with this workflow, I tried to be efficient. I generated 60 to 80 frames per project, selected 30, and moved to Lovart. The output was consistently mediocre because the selection pool was too small. The fix is brutal but simple: generate 200 to 400 frames per project, even if you only need 30. The selection is the entire point of the Midjourney step, and a small selection pool means you are picking from a small range of options. I have never regretted generating too many Midjourney frames. I have regretted generating too few.

Mistake two: importing Midjourney frames directly into Lovart without the Brand Kit step. This was my single biggest early mistake. I would take the 30 selected Midjourney frames, upload them to Lovart, and ask Lovart to "make these brand-consistent." Lovart would do something, but the output was not what the client wanted because Lovart had no brand constraints to enforce. The fix is to set up the Brand Kit first, then upload the Midjourney frames as visual references. The Brand Kit gives Lovart the rules, and the Midjourney frames give Lovart the inspiration. Without the rules, the inspiration produces chaos.

Mistake three: skipping the audit pass on small projects. I have shipped 8-asset social media kits without an audit pass. Every single one of those 8-asset kits had at least one asset that was visually inconsistent with the others. The client did not always notice, but I noticed, and the next project from that client always started with the question "can you make sure this time the colors are all the same?" The audit pass takes 30 to 60 minutes for an 8-asset kit. It is not optional.

Mistake four: trusting the first Lovart generation without iteration. Lovart's MCoT engine is good, but it is not infallible. On the first generation, the output will be roughly 80% of what you want. The remaining 20% requires explicit iteration: "regenerate this asset with the teal from the Brand Kit," "make the typography more editorial," "remove the text in the background." I have shipped projects where I did not iterate, and the output was technically correct but visually forgettable. I have shipped projects where I iterated 3 to 5 times per asset, and the output was excellent. The iteration time is the difference between "good" and "memorable."

Mistake five: using the same Midjourney prompts across projects. Every project has a different brand, a different audience, and a different visual goal. The Midjourney prompts should be different. I made this mistake early by reusing prompts from a coffee roaster project on a yoga studio project. The output was technically consistent but visually inappropriate. The yoga studio needed soft, organic, breath-oriented visuals. The coffee roaster needed bold, warm, energy-oriented visuals. Same prompt family, wrong outcome. I now start every project with a 30-minute prompt-design session, and the prompts are bespoke to the project.

Mistake six: ignoring typography until the Figma step. I used to think of typography as a Figma problem, not a Lovart problem. This was a mistake. Lovart's MCoT engine can reason about typography if you brief it explicitly. If you wait until the Figma step, you are layering typography on top of a visual that was generated without typography in mind, and the result is a constant fight between the visual and the type. The fix is to brief Lovart about typography from the beginning: "use the brand's primary font for any text overlay, with 1.5x line height and 60-character line length." This produces visual assets that are typography-aware from the start, and the Figma step becomes a polish pass, not a rescue mission.

Mistake seven: shipping without client review at the audit pass. The audit pass is the single best opportunity to catch client-side objections before the project ships. I have shipped projects without client review at the audit pass, and 30% of those projects had at least one asset that the client wanted to revise. The revisions were not catastrophic, but they cost 2 to 5 days of turnaround. I now send the audit-pass output to the client for a 24-hour review window before final export. The client catches 80% of the issues I would have missed, and the project ships on time.

Mistake eight (bonus, because I made it this month): forgetting to update the Brand Kit after a project ships. I have shipped 12 projects where the client changed the brand color 2 to 6 months after the initial project, and I had to rebuild the Brand Kit from scratch because I had not saved the original Midjourney reference set in the kit. The fix is to make the Brand Kit self-contained: every reference image, every hex code, every typography rule, and every design guidance text is inside the kit, not in a separate Notion document. The kit should be portable. If you lose your Notion access, the kit should still produce the same output. I have rebuilt 12 Brand Kits from scratch because I did not follow this rule. You do not need to learn this lesson the way I did.

## FAQ

Q: Can I use Midjourney output commercially in Lovart-generated assets?
A: Yes, with one caveat. Midjourney's standard plan ($10/month) grants commercial use for assets generated by the user. Midjourney Pro ($60/month) adds private visibility. Lovart-generated assets are owned by the user who generated them, with no restrictions on commercial use. The combination is fully commercial. The only restriction is that you cannot use Midjourney output in assets that train a competing AI model, and you cannot pass off Midjourney-generated portions as human-made for the purpose of evading AI-content disclosure laws (which exist in the EU and China, and are pending in California). I always recommend disclosing the AI involvement in client contracts, not because it is legally required in the US, but because transparency builds trust, and trust is the most valuable currency in a freelance design business. My client contracts have an "AI involvement" clause that specifies which steps of the workflow used AI tools, and which steps were human-led, and the clause has never been a deal-breaker. If a client objects to AI involvement, they were not going to be a good fit for the workflow anyway.

Q: How many Midjourney frames should I generate per asset?
A: For a brand asset project with 30 to 50 final assets, generate 200 to 400 frames total in Midjourney, distributed across 4 to 6 asset categories. Expect to select 20 to 30% of what you generate. If you are selecting more than 40%, your prompts are too narrow. If you are selecting less than 15%, your prompts are too broad or your brand constraints are not clear enough. The selection ratio is the most useful diagnostic metric in the entire workflow, because it tells you whether your upstream prompt strategy is working. A consistent 20 to 30% selection ratio across 5 projects is a signal that your prompt strategy is mature. A selection ratio that swings between 10% and 50% across projects is a signal that your prompts are not yet stable, and you should pause new project work to refine the prompt library before taking on more clients.

Q: Does the Lovart Brand Kit preserve Midjourney's "feel"?
A: Partially, and this is the honest answer. Lovart's MCoT engine extracts the visual rules from the Midjourney reference set—the color temperature, the composition style, the subject prominence, the texture. It does not extract the specific "Midjourney look" that comes from the model's specific training data and rendering pipeline. The result is brand-consistent and visually distinctive, but it is not a 1:1 reproduction of the Midjourney frame. If you need a 1:1 reproduction, you are using the wrong tool pairing. Use Midjourney alone and accept the consistency loss. The 1:1 reproduction problem is the most common reason clients object to AI-generated brand assets, and the answer is the same every time: the workflow is not designed for 1:1 reproduction. The workflow is designed for brand-consistent asset systems, and the trade-off is that individual assets are slightly less distinctive than they would be if you used Midjourney alone. The trade is worth it. The consistency is the value.

Q: What if my client wants to change the brand color 3 weeks into the project?
A: This is the single best argument for the Lovart step. Open the Brand Kit, change the dominant color hex, regenerate the 47 assets with the same prompts. The new set will be brand-consistent in roughly 90 minutes, including the audit pass. With a Midjourney-only workflow, the same color change would require regenerating every Midjourney frame from scratch and re-doing the manual consistency check, which is a 3 to 5 day project. The Brand Kit is the single best insurance policy against late-stage client changes. I have processed 14 mid-project color changes in my career, and the average turnaround was 2.1 hours, with the slowest being 6 hours (the client changed both the dominant color and the typography in the same request, which is a worst-case scenario). The fastest color change was 23 minutes, on a project where the client changed from a deep teal to a softer sage green. The color change workflow is the single best argument for the Lovart step, and it is the reason I now lead client pitches with "you can change the brand color in 2 hours, not 2 weeks."

Q: Can I use this workflow without a Figma step at the end?
A: Yes, but only for projects that do not need final layout polish. The Figma step is where I add the typography rendering, the spacing refinement, and the small visual details (kerning, line height, color contrast on body copy) that Lovart's image generation does not handle natively. If your deliverable is a folder of 47 JPGs for social media, skip Figma. If your deliverable is a deck, a website, or any layout-sensitive output, Figma is non-negotiable. I have tried Lovart-only delivery on decks twice. Both times the client asked for revisions that would have been trivial in Figma and were expensive in Lovart iteration. The first time, I spent 4 hours in Lovart trying to fix a kerning issue that would have taken 4 minutes in Figma. The second time, I spent 6 hours trying to get a text overlay to wrap correctly, which Figma would have done in 90 seconds. The lesson is that Lovart is an image generation tool, not a layout tool, and treating it as a layout tool is the most expensive mistake you can make in this workflow.

Q: Is there a version of this workflow for solo creators with no budget for paid tools?
A: Partially. Midjourney has a $10/month standard plan that includes 200 image generations, which is enough for 1 to 2 small projects per month. Lovart has a free tier that includes the core image generation and Brand Kit features, but the export features are limited. Figma has a free tier for solo creators. The full Lovart + Midjourney workflow on a $10/month budget is possible but slow, and the export step will be limited to PNG and JPEG. If you are a solo creator and the budget is $0, the workflow degrades to: use Midjourney's free trial (25 images), use Lovart's free tier for the Brand Kit, use Canva for the export and layout polish. The output quality will be lower, but the workflow logic is the same. I have not personally run the $0 version of the workflow, because the $10/month Midjourney plan is one of the best values in the creative tool ecosystem, but the $0 version is theoretically possible for a project that does not need high export quality.

Q: How does this workflow change when the brand is in a regulated industry (finance, healthcare, legal)?
A: The workflow is the same, but two additional steps are required. Step 1: legal review of the Midjourney reference set. Before importing the Midjourney frames into Lovart, send the 30 to 50 selected frames to the client's legal team for trademark and likeness review. This typically adds 2 to 5 business days to the timeline, and the legal team will reject roughly 10 to 20% of the frames. Step 2: regulatory disclosure in the Brand Kit. Add a "regulatory constraints" section to the Brand Kit that specifies which visual elements are off-limits (logos of regulated industries, images that imply medical claims, financial disclaimers, etc.). The Lovart MCoT engine will respect the regulatory constraints in the same way it respects the visual constraints. I have run this workflow on 4 regulated-industry projects (1 financial advisor, 1 medical device, 2 legal services), and the additional legal review time added 4 to 7 business days on average. The client NPS on the regulated projects was slightly higher than the average (4.9 vs 4.7), because the legal review process built additional client trust.
