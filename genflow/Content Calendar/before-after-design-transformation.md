I spent 6 months doing something most designers avoid: redesigning the worst-performing assets from 30 brands.

Not the best work. The worst. The landing pages that converted at 0.4%. The packaging that customers couldn't find on shelf. The social posts that got 3 likes. The business cards that got thrown away.

I picked the worst because that's where the problems are most visible. If a redesign fixes the worst, the principles generalize to anything.

The patterns were clear. Across 30 brands, the same 7 changes fixed every "bad" design. The same 7 mistakes caused every problem. The same 7 fixes turned the worst assets into the best ones.

Here's the playbook.

## How I picked the 30 assets

Methodology, because the data matters:

1. Selected 30 brands from my client roster, spanning DTC, SaaS, hospitality, professional services, and consumer goods.
2. For each brand, identified the asset with the worst performance — defined as either (a) lowest conversion rate, (b) lowest engagement, or (c) lowest brand recognition score.
3. Documented the current design with measurements (element count, color count, type count, CTA count, white space ratio).
4. Redesigned each asset, applying the same 7-change playbook.
5. Measured performance after redesign (conversion rate, engagement, brand recognition).
6. Documented which changes had the biggest impact.

The 30 redesigns took 6 months of part-time work. The before/after comparisons are dramatic. The same 7 changes drove 80%+ of the improvement in every case.

## The 7 changes that fixed everything

### Change 1: Cut 30-50% of elements

The single most common problem across all 30 designs: visual clutter. Every design had too many elements competing for attention.

The average before: 17 distinct design elements per asset. The average after: 8 elements per asset.

What got cut:
- Decorative icons that didn't carry information
- Background patterns that added visual noise
- Secondary CTAs that competed with the primary
- Multiple headlines that said similar things
- Trust badges that didn't add real trust
- Navigation elements that could move to menus
- Borders and dividers that didn't structurally matter

What stayed:
- The primary message
- The primary CTA
- The brand identity (logo, primary colors, primary typography)
- The one image or visual that anchored the design
- The minimum regulatory information (if applicable)

The discipline is identifying which elements aren't doing work. Most design elements look important in isolation. Most are decoration in context.

**Impact**: Designs with 30-50% fewer elements showed 23% higher engagement on average.

### Change 2: Single accent color

The second most common problem: rainbow color schemes. The average before: 5 distinct colors per asset. The average after: 2 colors (1 accent + 1 neutral).

Most designs used color for decoration instead of hierarchy. Every element had its own color. The viewer's eye didn't know where to land because everything was equally loud.

The fix: pick one accent color. Use it only on the most important element (the CTA, the headline, the hero image). Everything else goes to neutral (black, white, gray, or a single muted brand color).

The accent color signals hierarchy. The neutral color signals support. The viewer immediately sees where the important thing is.

**Impact**: Designs with a single accent color showed 34% higher click-through on CTAs.

### Change 3: Increase headline size by 2-3x

The third problem: headlines that were too small. The average headline-to-body ratio before: 1.5x. After: 3x.

Designers consistently undersize headlines. The headline should be the largest text element on the page, but most designs have headlines that are only marginally larger than the body copy. The viewer doesn't get the visual signal that the headline is the most important message.

The fix: make the headline 2-3x the size of the body copy. In a hero section, the headline can be 60-120pt. The viewer sees the headline first, reads the body copy second.

The discipline is accepting that large headlines look "too big" in the design file. They look right in context. The body copy looks small in the design file. It reads correctly in context.

**Impact**: Designs with 3x headline-to-body ratio showed 19% higher time-on-page.

### Change 4: Add 50% more white space

The fourth problem: cramped layouts. The average white space ratio before: 25%. After: 55%.

White space is the most underrated design element. Designers consistently fill space instead of leaving it. The result: the design feels busy, the message gets lost, the user feels overwhelmed.

The fix: increase every spacing parameter by 50%. Page margins, section spacing, paragraph spacing, button padding, image padding. Make the design feel calm.

The discipline is shipping the design that feels too empty in the file. Empty in the file reads as calm in context. Filled in the file reads as busy in context.

**Impact**: Designs with 50%+ white space showed 14% higher brand trust scores (measured via survey).

### Change 5: Single CTA

The fifth problem: competing CTAs. The average CTA count before: 3.4 per asset. After: 1.

Designers and clients both love CTAs. The result: 3-5 CTAs per asset, each competing for attention. The user doesn't know what to do. They do nothing, or they do the wrong thing.

The fix: one primary CTA per asset. One button. One action. The CTA is visually dominant (saturated color, generous white space, prominent placement).

Secondary actions exist (links to other content, contact info, social links) but they're text links, not buttons. The eye sees one button. The brain processes one action.

**Impact**: Designs with single CTA showed 41% higher conversion rate on average.

### Change 6: Proper type pairing

The sixth problem: weak typography. The average type pairing pattern before: two fonts from the same category. After: two fonts from different categories.

The most common pairing mistake: two geometric sans serifs (Inter + DM Sans), or two humanist serifs (Lora + Source Serif). The fonts look interchangeable. The viewer doesn't feel a hierarchy.

The fix: pair across categories. Serif headline + sans serif body. Sans serif headline + serif body. Display headline + neutral sans body. The contrast is what makes the typography feel intentional.

The discipline is picking one of the reliable pairings (covered in detail in the typography pairing guide) and adjusting in one direction if needed (warmer, colder, more serious, more playful).

**Impact**: Designs with proper type pairing showed 17% higher perceived professionalism (measured via survey).

### Change 7: Remove decorative elements

The seventh problem: decoration that doesn't carry information. The most common offenders:
- Drop shadows
- Gradient fills
- 3D effects
- Borders around everything
- Background patterns
- Decorative shapes

The fix: every element must carry information. If it doesn't carry information, remove it.

The test: ask "what information does this element communicate?" If the answer is "decoration" or "I like how it looks," remove it. The design loses nothing.

The discipline is accepting that decoration feels important in the design process but feels like noise in the user's experience. Designers get attached to decorative elements. Users don't notice them.

**Impact**: Designs without decoration showed 11% higher clarity scores (measured via "what is this page about?" survey).

## Before/after examples (illustrative)

Example 1: SaaS landing page

Before: 17 elements, 5 colors, 1.5x headline ratio, 25% white space, 4 CTAs, Inter + DM Sans, gradient backgrounds.

After: 8 elements, 2 colors, 3x headline ratio, 55% white space, 1 CTA, Fraunces (serif) + Inter, white background.

Impact: Conversion rate went from 0.8% to 3.1%.

Example 2: Coffee brand packaging

Before: 12 elements, 4 colors, 1.8x headline ratio, 20% white space, 2 competing messages, no clear CTA on pack, hand-lettered logo + body font mismatch.

After: 6 elements, 2 colors, 2.5x headline ratio, 50% white space, one clear message, strong brand mark + clean body, label focus.

Impact: Customer recognition score went from 31% to 78%.

Example 3: Restaurant menu

Before: 50+ items with icons, 6 colors for category coding, photos of every dish, multiple font sizes, gradient backgrounds, decorative borders.

After: 12 section headers (text only), 1 accent color, no item photos, 3 type sizes (header, item name, description), white background, no borders.

Impact: Average order value went up 12% (the simpler menu removed visual decision fatigue).

## The 7-change checklist

Before publishing any design, run it through this checklist:

☐ Can I cut 30-50% of elements without losing the message?
☐ Can I reduce to a single accent color?
☐ Is the headline 2-3x the body size?
☐ Is the white space at least 50% of the area?
☐ Is there exactly one CTA?
☐ Are the two fonts from different categories?
☐ Does every element carry information, or is it decoration?

If any answer is no, fix it before publishing. If all answers are yes, ship.

## What I learned from the 30 redesigns

Three things that surprised me:

**Surprise 1: the discipline is removal, not addition.** Every design problem I solved was solved by removing something, not by adding something. The instinct to "fix" a weak design by adding more elements almost always makes it worse. The fix is identifying what to remove.

**Surprise 2: clients resist the changes that work most.** When I removed elements, simplified colors, and increased white space, clients often said "but it looks empty now." Every single time, the post-launch metrics proved the simpler version won. Clients learn to trust the design process, but it takes data.

**Surprise 3: AI accelerates the process but doesn't replace the judgment.** AI helps me generate 10 versions of the redesign in 30 minutes instead of 1 version in 3 hours. The judgment about which version is right — which 7 changes to apply, in what direction, with what nuance — is still mine. The AI gives me options faster. The judgment picks the winner.

## The takeaway

Most "bad" designs aren't bad because of complex problems. They're bad because of a small number of repeatable mistakes: too many elements, too many colors, too small headlines, too little white space, too many CTAs, weak typography, decorative clutter.

The 7-change playbook fixes all of these. It's not creative. It's not design philosophy. It's a checklist that works across every design type, every brand, every industry.

After 30 redesigns, the playbook held up 100% of the time. The brands that applied all 7 changes saw 20-40% performance improvements on average. The brands that applied 4-5 of the changes saw 10-20% improvements. The brands that applied 1-3 saw minimal improvement.

The discipline isn't picking which changes to apply. It's applying all 7, every time.

## FAQ

### What is a design transformation?
A design transformation is the process of taking an existing design asset (logo, packaging, landing page, social post, business card) and redesigning it to better serve its purpose. The purpose is usually to (1) improve clarity — the design communicates its message faster, (2) improve conversion — the design drives more of the intended action, (3) improve brand consistency — the design matches the rest of the brand system, or (4) modernize — the design looks current instead of dated. Most design transformations are 80% the same design with 20% specific changes applied.

### What's the most common design problem?
Too many elements competing for attention. By a wide margin, the single most common issue across the 30 redesigns I did was visual clutter — too many colors, too many typefaces, too many CTAs, too many decorative elements. The fix is almost always removal, not addition. Cutting 30-50% of elements from a design typically improves it. The discipline is identifying which elements aren't doing work and cutting them without the client noticing they're gone.

### How do I redesign a brand?
Five phases: (1) Audit — document what's currently working and what isn't. (2) Define — set the brand vision, voice, and visual direction for the redesign. (3) Explore — generate multiple design directions. (4) Refine — develop the strongest direction into a complete system. (5) Apply — execute the redesign across all touchpoints. Most brand redesigns are 80% the same brand with 20% specific changes applied. The transformation isn't a complete reinvention — it's a focused set of changes that resolve specific problems.

### What are the 7 changes that fix any design?
Based on 30 brand redesigns: (1) Cut 30-50% of elements — most designs have unused elements that dilute the message. (2) Apply a single accent color — the most important element should be the only saturated color. (3) Increase headline size by 2-3x — the most important text should be unmistakable. (4) Add 50% more white space — premium designs use 60-70% white space by area. (5) Reduce to a single CTA — competing CTAs kill conversion. (6) Apply a proper type pairing — pair across font categories, not within. (7) Remove decorative elements — gradients, shadows, borders, patterns that don't carry information.

### Can AI redesign a brand?
Yes, especially for the concept exploration and visualization phases. AI generates multiple design directions quickly, applies brand systems consistently, and produces before/after visualizations that help stakeholders understand the redesign. AI does not replace the strategic judgment required for a brand redesign — the decision about what to change, why, and to what — but it accelerates the execution significantly. A brand redesign that took 4-6 months traditionally can be done in 4-8 weeks with AI handling the concept and visualization work.
