## Episode Overview

Zoe Kim started Nura Home in her Brooklyn apartment in 2025 with $15,000 in savings, a single product (premium linen bedding), and a belief that AI design tools could level the playing field against established home goods brands with million-dollar creative budgets. Two years later, Nura Home does $10 million in annual revenue, sells into Nordstrom and Anthropologie, and still operates with a creative team of just three people — all powered by AI. In this episode, Zoe shares her playbook for using AI design to punch above your weight class in e-commerce.

## Full Transcript

### [00:00] Introduction

**Marcus Chen:** Welcome back to the Lovart Podcast. I'm Marcus Chen. Today's guest is Zoe Kim, founder and CEO of Nura Home — a DTC home goods brand that's done something remarkable. In under two years, Nura Home went from zero to $10 million in annual revenue, got into Nordstrom and Anthropologie, and did it all with a creative team of just three people and AI design tools as their force multiplier. Zoe, welcome.

**Zoe Kim:** Thanks, Marcus. Happy to be here.

**Marcus:** Let's start at the beginning. You were at Wayfair before this. What made you leave to start Nura Home?

**Zoe:** I was a product manager at Wayfair, working on the search and discovery experience. I spent every day looking at what sold in home goods and why. One thing kept jumping out at me: the brands winning weren't necessarily the ones with the best products. They were the ones with the best visual presentation. Better photography, better lifestyle imagery, better brand storytelling. And that visual advantage cost money — big photoshoot budgets, big creative teams, big agencies. I thought: what if AI could collapse that cost? What if a solo founder could create the visual quality of a venture-backed brand? That was the thesis for Nura Home.

**Marcus:** And you tested it with a single product. What was the launch like?

**Zoe:** Linen bedding. One SKU, four colors. I had $15,000. Traditional product launch would have consumed that entire budget just on photography — model shoot, lifestyle shoot, flat lays, detail shots, room settings. I spent $300 on actual product samples and did everything else with AI.

For our launch photos, I generated every image — bedroom settings with the bedding, lifestyle shots, detail close-ups, even "model" images with the bedding draped artistically. It took me three days and about $50 in AI generation credits to create what would have been a $12,000 photoshoot. And honestly? The AI images tested better. They had a consistent, dreamy aesthetic that stood out against the overly lit, overly staged look of traditional home goods photography.

We sold out our first production run in 11 days.

### [09:15] The Visual Strategy

**Marcus:** What was the visual strategy? How did you make Nura Home look different from every other linen bedding brand?

**Zoe:** We made three deliberate decisions.

**First:** We rejected the "perfect bedroom" look. Traditional home goods photography shows immaculate bedrooms that no real person lives in. Our imagery shows beds that look slept in — beautiful but believable. Slightly rumpled linen, morning light, a book on the nightstand, a half-drunk cup of tea. It's aspirational but attainable. AI is actually great at this because you can prompt for "lived-in luxury" — a concept that's hard to art direct on a traditional photoshoot.

**Second:** We built a consistent color world. Every Nura Home image — whether it's a product shot, a social post, or an email — exists within a specific color palette: warm creams, soft earth tones, muted blues, and terracotta accents. We configured this in Lovart's Brand Kit once, and now every AI-generated image automatically stays within our color world. That consistency is what makes a three-person brand look like a 50-person brand.

**Third:** We showed texture obsessively. Home goods are tactile products. You can't touch them online — but you can simulate the experience through extreme close-up photography. We use AI macro photography prompts to generate images that show the weave of the linen, the weight of the fabric, the way light plays across the texture. Customers routinely tell us "the product looks exactly like the photos" — and that's because we showed them what the product actually feels like, not just what it looks like.

### [14:00] The Three-Person Creative Machine

**Marcus:** Walk me through your team. Three people doing the work of fifteen. How?

**Zoe:** We have me (CEO, brand strategy, high-level creative direction), a creative lead (manages all visual output, writes prompts, curates AI generations), and a growth marketer (runs ads, email, social, analytics).

On a typical week, we produce:
- 20-25 social media posts across Instagram, TikTok, Pinterest, and Facebook
- 5-7 ad creative variants for ongoing A/B tests
- 3 emails with unique visual content
- Product listing updates and seasonal refreshes
- Wholesale line sheets and lookbooks for retail partners

That's roughly 35-40 unique visual assets per week. With a traditional team, that's 3-4 designers full-time. With AI, it's one creative lead spending about 25 hours a week on generation and curation.

**Marcus:** What does the creative lead's workflow actually look like?

**Zoe:** Monday morning: they review the content calendar and write prompts for the week's assets. By Monday afternoon, Lovart has generated 200-300 images across all the content types. Tuesday is curation — going through the outputs, selecting the best, flagging ones that need refinement. Wednesday is refinement — taking the selected images and iterating with more specific prompts. Thursday is finalization and export. Friday is scheduling and publishing.

The key is that our creative lead never starts from a blank canvas. They always start from dozens of AI-generated options and make decisions. The skill isn't in making images — it's in knowing which images are right for the brand.

### [18:45] Product Photography at AI Speed

**Marcus:** You mentioned earlier that AI photography tested better than traditional photos. Tell me more about that.

**Zoe:** We actually ran a split test. Same product, same listing. Variant A used traditional product photography from a professional shoot we eventually did when we had more budget. Variant B used AI-generated photography. The AI-generated images had a 14% higher conversion rate.

I think there are two reasons. One, consistency — the AI images all exist in the same "world" because they're generated from the same visual parameters. Traditional photoshoots, even with the best art direction, have slight inconsistencies in lighting, color temperature, and styling across shots. Two, idealism — AI can generate the platonic ideal of a bedroom setting in a way that's very difficult and expensive to achieve in a real photoshoot. The lighting is always perfect. The styling is always aspirational. Nothing is out of place unless you want it to be.

These days, we use traditional photography only for authenticity moments — behind-the-scenes content, founder shots, UGC. Everything else is AI-generated.

### [23:30] A/B Testing at Scale

**Marcus:** You mentioned testing 50 ad creatives per week. How does that work with a three-person team?

**Zoe:** It's the only way it works. Our growth marketer identifies what variables we want to test — headline style, image type (lifestyle vs. product close-up vs. room setting), color palette, benefit messaging. Our creative lead generates the ad variants using Lovart's batch generation. We typically test 10-15 variants per ad set, 3-4 ad sets per week. So 30-60 creative variants total.

A traditional team would need a dedicated performance creative designer just for this. We do it in about 3 hours of the creative lead's time per week. And because we test so many variants, we find winners faster. Our average ad creative fatigue cycle is about 3 weeks — for competitors running 3-5 variants, it's more like 5-7 days.

**Marcus:** That's a massive competitive advantage.

**Zoe:** It's the single biggest advantage AI gives us. Not cost savings — competitive speed. We can test more ideas, fail faster on the bad ones, and scale the winners before competitors even finish their first round of creative development.

### [33:15] Getting Into Nordstrom and Anthropologie

**Marcus:** Wholesale is a different game. What did those retailers require visually that was different from DTC?

**Zoe:** Wholesale buyers need to visualize your product in their context — on their website, in their stores, next to their other brands. We generated custom line sheets and lookbooks for each retailer pitch. For Nordstrom, we showed our bedding in bedroom settings that matched Nordstrom's visual style guide. For Anthropologie, we leaned into their more eclectic, artisanal aesthetic.

Both buyers explicitly asked during the pitch process, "How do you produce this many visual assets as a new brand?" We were transparent about using AI, and both buyers were completely fine with it — as long as the product photography was accurate. They cared about whether the images would sell products on their platforms, not how the images were made.

The irony is that AI-enabled visual consistency actually made us a more attractive wholesale partner. Retailers hate when a brand's imagery is inconsistent across SKUs because it makes their site look messy. Our AI-generated catalog is perfectly consistent, which reduces their merchandising work.

### [41:00] Advice for DTC Founders

**Marcus:** Final question. A founder listening to this is about to launch a DTC brand with $20,000. What do you tell them about visual strategy?

**Zoe:** Five things.

**One:** Spend your money on product, not photography. Your first photoshoot should be $0. Use AI. When you hit $1M in revenue, then you can hire a photographer if you still want to.

**Two:** Consistency beats creativity. A perfectly consistent visual identity that's "good enough" will outperform occasional moments of creative brilliance wrapped in visual inconsistency. Focus on building your Brand Kit first, then generate.

**Three:** Test everything. The beauty of AI is that creative experimentation is almost free. If you have a hunch that warm-toned images might convert better than cool-toned ones, generate both and test. Don't guess — know.

**Four:** Show texture. I cannot emphasize this enough for physical products. Close-up texture photography builds trust. It tells the customer "this is real, this is quality, you know exactly what you're getting."

**Five:** Be transparent about AI. Customers don't care how the image was made. They care whether the product matches the image. If your AI photography accurately represents your product, no one will complain. If it doesn't, no amount of disclosure will save you.

**Marcus:** Zoe Kim, founder of Nura Home. Thank you for the masterclass.

**Zoe:** Thanks, Marcus. This was fun.

**Marcus:** That's Episode 3 of the Lovart Podcast. Next episode, we're talking with a design educator about how design schools are — and aren't — adapting to AI. See you in a week.
