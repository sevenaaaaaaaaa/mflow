# How to Design a Brand for Your Small Business with AI — Complete Startup Guide

[IMAGE 1 PLACEHOLDER — Persona Scenario]

You registered the LLC. You have a product or service people might actually pay for. Now you need a brand. Not a logo — a brand. The visual system that makes your bakery look different from the bakery next door, your consulting practice feel established instead of accidental.

Agencies charge $3,000 to $15,000 for what I'm about to show you. Graphic designers on Fiverr charge $200 to $800. Both options are legitimate. But when you're bootstrapping, $800 is inventory. It's a month of Google Ads. It's the difference between launching in March and launching in May.

AI brand design isn't about replacing designers. It's about giving you an option that didn't exist before: professional-grade visual identity at zero marginal cost, built on your timeline.

## What "Brand" Actually Means (The Parts List)

[IMAGE 2 PLACEHOLDER — Conceptual Diagram]

Before you generate anything, know what you're building. A brand identity has seven components:

| Component | What It Is | Deliverable |
|-----------|-----------|-------------|
| Logo | Primary mark, no text needed | PNG, SVG, favicon |
| Wordmark | Your business name in styled type | PNG, SVG |
| Color palette | 3-5 hex codes you use everywhere | Swatch sheet |
| Typography | 1-2 fonts for all materials | Font files or names |
| Iconography | Small visual elements | Icon set |
| Photography style | The look of your images | Style guide examples |
| Templates | Social, print, web layouts | Reusable files |

Most solopreneurs stop at the logo. That's like building a house and only installing the front door. The full system is what makes customers think "these people have their act together."

## Step 1: The Logo and Wordmark

Start here because everything else references it. Your logo sets the tone, the shapes, the level of sophistication.

**The AI logo workflow on Lovart:**

1. Describe your business in one sentence. "A sourdough bakery in Portland that delivers subscriptions in mason jars." Not "artisanal baked goods purveyor." Be concrete.

2. Write a logo prompt using this formula:

```
[Style] logo for [business type] called "[name]," [key visual element], [vibe adjectives], [color preference], clean vector-style, white background
```

Example: "Minimalist logo for a bakery called 'Jar & Loaf,' wheat stalk forming a jar silhouette, warm and rustic, amber and cream, clean vector-style, white background."

3. Generate 8-10 variations. Pick 3 that feel directionally right. Generate variations of those 3. Narrow to 1.

4. For the wordmark, prompt separately: "Wordmark typography for [business name], [font style], [alignment with logo style], transparent background." The wordmark needs to work independently from the icon.

Pro tip: Generate your logo in black first. A logo that works in pure black and white will work anywhere — embroidery, screen printing, favicon, invoice header. If it only looks good in color, it's fragile.

## Step 2: The Color Palette (The 60-30-10 Rule)

You need five colors. Not three. Not twelve. Five.

- **Primary (60%):** The dominant color. Backgrounds, large areas.
- **Secondary (30%):** The supporting color. Headers, buttons, accents.
- **Accent (10%):** The pop. CTAs, highlights, energy.
- **Dark neutral:** For text and depth.
- **Light neutral:** For backgrounds and contrast.

Don't guess. Generate a color palette image: "Color palette swatches for [business type], modern brand identity, [mood], numbered hex codes, professional brand guide layout." Lovart can output palette-style images you reference when designing everything else.

David in Portland runs a lawn care company. His palette: forest green (#2D5A27), warm sand (#E8D5B7), bright lime accent (#A8D147), charcoal text (#2B2B2B), off-white background (#F7F3EB). He didn't choose these — he generated a palette from a description of "earthy, trustworthy, fresh" and got a set that looks like it came from a design studio.

## Step 3: Typography That Doesn't Scream "Default"

[IMAGE 3 PLACEHOLDER — Real UI Screenshot]

Most AI-generated designs use whatever the model defaults to. That usually means something bland and vaguely sans-serif. Take control.

**Typography pairing formula:**
- Headline font: Distinctive (serif, display, or bold sans-serif)
- Body font: Invisible (clean, readable sans-serif or serif)
- Never use more than 2 fonts across your entire brand

Prompt for typography inspiration: "Typography pairing for [business type], [mood], headline and body text, editorial design sample." Use the output as reference, not the literal typeface — you'll source actual fonts from Google Fonts or Adobe Fonts based on what the AI suggests.

Google Fonts pairings that match common AI suggestions:
- Playfair Display + Source Sans Pro (elegant, editorial)
- Montserrat + Merriweather (modern, warm)
- Bebas Neue + Roboto (bold, urban)
- Cormorant Garamond + Proza Libre (classic, literary)

## Step 4: Templates — The Part That Saves You 100 Hours

A logo and palette are assets. Templates are production. Build these five first:

**Social media template set.** One square post layout, one story layout. Same placement of logo, same color scheme, swappable imagery. Generate the base layout, then duplicate for each platform dimension.

**Business card.** Generate one, not ten. "Modern minimalist business card, [color palette], [business name], [title], [contact info], clean layout, print-ready, white border."

**Email signature.** Small, functional, everywhere. Generate the graphic elements (logo mark, divider line in brand colors), assemble in your email client.

**Invoice or quote template.** Looks minor, affects every client interaction. "Clean invoice template, [brand colors], modern typography, professional but friendly, white background, clear hierarchy."

**Presentation deck cover.** If you ever pitch anything, you need this. "Presentation cover slide, [brand colors], bold title typography, [business name], clean modern layout, professional."

Lovart saves these as projects you can revisit. When you need a new Instagram post in three months, you open the template, swap the imagery, and export — rather than designing from zero every time.

## Step 5: Photography Style Definition

Consistency in photography matters more than individual image quality. Define the style once:

Prompt: "Brand photography moodboard for [business type], [lighting style], [color treatment], [subject matter], cohesive editorial aesthetic."

Examples:
- Bakery: "Warm natural light, overhead flat lay, slightly desaturated, linen textures, hands in frame"
- Consulting: "Clean office environments, soft window light, warm neutrals, genuine expressions, shallow depth of field"
- Fitness: "High contrast, dynamic motion, sweat and grit, natural light, urban environments"

This prompt becomes your reference. Every product photo, every team headshot, every Instagram post gets filtered through these adjectives. It's not about generating every photo with AI — it's about having a clear brief for whatever imagery you create or commission.

## FAQ

**Q: Can I trademark an AI-generated logo?**
Trademark law protects logos as commercial identifiers regardless of how they were created. However, you should ensure the AI-generated logo is sufficiently distinctive. Avoid generic geometric marks. Consult a trademark attorney — the AI tool is for creation, not legal advice.

**Q: What if I hate everything the AI generates?**
Your prompts are probably too vague. "Modern logo for my business" produces generic results because it's a generic prompt. Add specifics: industry, name, visual metaphor, vibe, colors. A bad prompt produces bad output the same way a bad brief produces bad agency work.

**Q: How do I get SVG versions of AI-generated designs for print?**
AI image generators output raster images (PNG, JPEG). For vector conversion (SVG), use a vectorization tool. Lovart includes export options that integrate with vector trace workflows. For simple logo marks, free online SVG converters work well.

**Q: Do I own the brand assets I create with AI?**
On Lovart's Free plan, outputs are for personal use. On paid plans ($19/mo and above), you receive full commercial rights. This means you can use the logo, templates, and imagery in your business, register trademarks, and print materials without restriction.

**Q: How many logo variations do I need?**
At minimum: primary logo (icon + wordmark), icon-only version (for favicon, app icon), wordmark-only (for headers, invoices), and a white/reversed version (for dark backgrounds). Four files. Same logo, different configurations.

**Q: What's the difference between a brand and a brand kit?**
A brand is the full visual and emotional identity system. A brand kit is the folder of assets (logo files, color hex codes, font names, templates). You can build a brand kit in a weekend. Building a brand — the reputation, the recognition, the associations — takes years. The kit is the foundation.

**Q: Can I use AI for brand naming too, or just visuals?**
Yes. Prompt Lovart or any LLM: "Generate 20 business name ideas for a [business type] that does [specific service] in [location]. Names should feel [3 adjectives]. Avoid puns. Prefer real words over made-up ones." AI naming works best as idea generation — you still need to check domain availability and trademark conflicts yourself.
