## The Exact Amazon Requirements (What Gets Your Listing Suppressed)

Before you generate anything, understand what Amazon's image moderation algorithm is looking for. I learned most of this the hard way — by getting listings rejected and reading the appeals docs at 2am.

### Main Image Requirements (Non-Negotiable)

**Pure white background.** RGB 255,255,255. Not "off-white." Not "light gray." Not "whitish with a gradient." Amazon's moderation tool samples background pixels. If they don't read as 255,255,255, the image gets rejected. I tested this with a "subtle cream" background once because I thought it looked softer. Rejected in 11 minutes.

**Product fills at least 85% of the frame.** The product should dominate the image. Excess negative space triggers the "product not prominently displayed" rejection. Some sellers report getting away with 80%, but I wouldn't risk it. When I generate Amazon main images, I aim for the product taking up 88-92% of the frame height.

**No text, logos, watermarks, or graphics on the image.** Brand logos can appear on the product itself (like a logo printed on a box) but not as an overlay on the photo. No "Best Seller" badges, no "New!" callouts, no promotional banners. Pure product only.

**No props or accessories.** If it's not in the box, it's not in the photo. A phone case photographed next to a phone that's not included will get rejected. A yoga mat with a water bottle in frame — rejected. I tested the boundary here with a bundle product (a shaving kit with a stand) and Amazon rejected the stand because it was listed as a separate SKU. Lesson learned.

**No lifestyle context.** The product is shown as a product. Not the product in use, being held, or placed in a room. That's what your alternative images are for. Amazon is very strict on this — I once got a rejection for a stainless steel water bottle where the cap was slightly unscrewed and a tiny wisp of "steam" was visible. Apparently that counted as "in use."

**Image dimensions.** At least 1000 pixels on the longest side (ideally 1600px or larger for the zoom feature). Square format (1:1) is standard, though 4:5 vertical and 1.91:1 horizontal are allowed. I stick to 2000x2000px for everything — it gives me room to crop and still meet the 1600px threshold.

**File format.** JPEG, PNG, or TIFF. I use PNG for products with transparent edges (though you shouldn't need transparency for main images since the background should be solid white) and JPEG for everything else to save space.

### Recommendations for Alternative Images (Compliance Not Required, Visibility Strongly Recommended)

Amazon allows up to 9 images (1 main image + 8 alternatives). The alternatives can and should include lifestyle context, size comparisons, feature highlights, packaging shots, and application demonstrations. The main image serves search results and product identification. The alternatives serve conversion. I'll get into how I use AI to generate both later in this guide.

For alternative images, the rules loosen up. You can have text overlays, lifestyle settings, people using the product, infographics with measurements, comparison charts — almost anything that helps the customer understand the product. But they still can't be misleading, and Amazon has been cracking down on "too much text" in recent updates. I keep text to under 30% of the frame for alternative images to be safe.

## The Step-by-Step Workflow: How I Generate Amazon Main Images with Lovart

Now that you know which tools I recommend and why, here's the exact workflow I use to produce Amazon-compliant main images.

### Step 1: Photograph Your Actual Product (Even Badly)

You need a reference image of your real product. I know I keep saying this, but it's the single most important step. The AI needs to know what your product actually looks like — not a generic version of a product in your category.

You don't need professional equipment. I use my iPhone 13 with natural window light. I put the product on a clean white surface (a piece of printer paper works). I take 3-5 photos from slightly different angles. I pick the sharpest one and upload it to Lovart.

Some tips for the reference photo:
- Use even, diffused lighting (window light on an overcast day is perfect)
- Avoid harsh shadows
- Make sure the product fills at least 50% of the frame
- Shoot straight-on or at a 3/4 angle (not from above or below)
- Use a contrasting background if your product is white (so the AI can detect the edges)

### Step 2: Set Up Your ChatCanvas

In Lovart, I create a new ChatCanvas project for each product. I drag my reference photo into the canvas and label it clearly (e.g., "actual_product_reference").

I also set up my Brand Kit at this point if I haven't already. This includes:
- Brand colors (for alternative images, not main images)
- Logo file (in case I need it for infographics)
- Typography preferences
- Any specific style notes (e.g., "minimalist," "premium feel")

### Step 3: Write the Main Image Prompt

Here's the exact prompt structure I use for Amazon main images. Save this as a template:

```
@reference [your_product_reference.jpg]

Amazon main image. This exact product on pure white background (RGB 255,255,255). Product fills 85% of frame. Professional studio lighting — soft, diffused, no harsh shadows. Product accurately represented — same shape, proportions, and details as the reference. Centered composition. Slight 3/4 angle to show depth. High resolution, 2000x2000px. No text, no logos overlaid, no props, no lifestyle context. Photorealistic product photography.
```

Some variations I use:
- For flat products (like my phone wallet), I use a top-down angle: "Overhead shot, product fills 85% of frame"
- For tall products (like a water bottle), I use a slight low angle: "Product shot from slightly below center, product fills 85% of frame vertically"
- For sets (like my drawer organizers), I specify the arrangement: "Four organizers nested as shown in reference, arranged in a 2x2 grid"

### Step 4: Generate, Review, and Iterate

I usually generate 4 variations at once. Then I review them against Amazon's requirements:

- Background reads as pure 255,255,255? (I check by sampling pixels in Photoshop)
- Product fills at least 85% of the frame? (I measure with the crop tool)
- Product matches my reference? (I compare side-by-side)
- No text, logos, or props? (Visual check)
- Lighting is even and professional? (Visual check)
- No visible artifacts or AI weirdness? (Visual check)

If none of the 4 variations pass, I refine my prompt and try again. If one is close but has a minor issue, I use Touch Edit to fix it. Common Touch Edit fixes:
- "Remove faint shadow on left side"
- "Center product more precisely"
- "Brighten background to pure white"
- "Show handle detail more clearly"

### Step 5: Export and Verify

I export the final image as a 2000x2000px PNG. Before uploading to Amazon, I verify:
- File size is under Amazon's 10MB limit (usually it's around 2-4MB for a PNG)
- Color profile is sRGB
- Resolution is exactly 2000x2000 (or whatever dimensions I chose)
- File format is JPEG or PNG

Then I upload through Seller Central and wait for the moderation verdict. With Lovart-generated images using this workflow, I get approval on the first try about 95% of the time.

## Pitfalls I Hit (And How to Avoid Them)

Let me save you some of the pain I went through. These are the mistakes that cost me the most time and money.

### Pitfall 1: Generic Products Instead of Your Actual Product

The most common mistake is generating "a product like yours" instead of "your specific product." I made this mistake early on. I generated a beautiful main image of a coffee mug that looked nothing like the mug I was actually selling. The handle was different, the logo was missing, the proportions were off.

The fix: Always use a reference image of your actual product. The @reference feature in ChatCanvas is there for a reason. Use it.

### Pitfall 2: Backgrounds That Look White But Aren't

This one is sneaky. The background looks pure white on your screen. You export it. You upload it. Amazon rejects it. You check the image file in Photoshop and... the background is RGB 254,254,254. Not 255,255,255.

AI image generators often produce backgrounds that are "almost white" but not exactly white. Lovart's MCoT Engine is specifically trained to produce exact 255,255,255 backgrounds, which is why it passes moderation more reliably. But even with Lovart, I always verify the background pixel values before uploading.

The fix: Sample the background pixels in any image editor. If they're not all reading as 255,255,255, use Touch Edit to fix or regenerate with a more specific prompt.

### Pitfall 3: Shadows That Cross the Background

Amazon's moderation will reject images with visible shadows on the background — even soft, subtle ones. The logic is that a shadow makes the background not "pure white." Some shadows are so faint you don't notice them until Amazon's algorithm does.

The fix: In your prompt, explicitly request "no shadows on background" or "floating product, no ground shadow." If shadows appear in the output, use Touch Edit to remove them or regenerate.

### Pitfall 4: Product Fill Ratio Under 85%

I had this issue with my drawer organizer set. I generated an image where the organizers were clearly visible but took up maybe 75% of the frame. I thought it looked "airy" and "elegant." Amazon thought it was "product not prominently displayed."

The fix: Measure the product in the frame. The product should occupy at least 85% of the image height (or width, whichever is larger). When in doubt, zoom in more. You can always show more of the product; you can't show less after Amazon rejects it.

### Pitfall 5: Inconsistent Products Across Images

This is a subtle one that doesn't cause Amazon rejections but does hurt conversion. If your main image shows a mug with a specific handle shape, and your lifestyle image shows a mug with a slightly different handle, customers notice. They might not consciously register it, but the inconsistency creates doubt: "Is this the same product?"

The fix: Use Identity Lock in Lovart to maintain product consistency across all generated images. Generate all images in a single ChatCanvas session using the same reference photo.

### Pitfall 6: Text in the Wrong Place

Amazon doesn't allow text on main images. I once generated a main image where the AI helpfully added a small "100%" badge on the product packaging (it interpreted the packaging design as needing a label). Rejected.

The fix: Explicitly say "no text, no labels, no graphics" in your prompt. If text appears, use Touch Edit to remove it or regenerate.

### Pitfall 7: Wrong Product Color or Material

AI image generators sometimes "interpret" your prompt in unexpected ways. I asked for a "matte black" phone wallet and got a glossy black one. I asked for "brushed steel" organizers and got polished chrome. These are rejection-worthy because they don't match the product you're actually selling.

The fix: Be very specific about material and finish in your prompt. "Matte finish, no reflections" or "brushed stainless steel, not polished." Reference images help here too — the AI can see the actual finish in your photo.

## International Amazon Marketplaces: Do Requirements Differ?

I'm often asked whether Amazon's image requirements differ across marketplaces (US, UK, Germany, Japan, etc.). Short answer: mostly no, with some edge cases.

### What's Consistent Across Marketplaces

- Pure white background (RGB 255,255,255) — universal
- Product fills 85% of frame — universal
- No text, logos, or graphics on main image — universal
- No props or lifestyle context on main image — universal
- Minimum 1000px on longest side — universal

### What Differs

- **Japan (Amazon.co.jp)**: Slightly more lenient on shadow presence; some lifestyle elements are tolerated on main images
- **Germany (Amazon.de)**: Stricter on claims; anything that could be considered a health or efficacy claim on packaging is scrutinized
- **UK (Amazon.co.uk)**: Same as US, post-Brexit
- **India (Amazon.in)**: Stricter on text in alternative images; they enforce the 30% text limit more aggressively

For most sellers, generating to the strictest standard (US) will pass everywhere. Don't optimize for the loosest marketplace.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Is using AI for Amazon product images actually allowed?

A: Yes, as of 2026, Amazon does not prohibit AI-generated product images, provided they meet all the standard image requirements (pure white background, 85% fill ratio, no text overlays, accurate product representation). Amazon has hinted at future disclosure requirements, but currently no specific labeling is mandatory. The key requirement is that the generated image accurately represents the product the customer will receive. Misleading AI-generated images (showing a different product than what's sold) violate Amazon's existing policies regardless of whether AI was used.

### Q: How does Lovart compare to hiring a professional product photographer?

A: For pure white background main images that meet Amazon's requirements, Lovart produces results comparable to a professional product photographer at a fraction of the cost and time. The break-even point is around 10-20 products. Below that, a photographer might be more cost-effective. Above that, AI generation scales better. For lifestyle and creative alternative images, a professional photographer still has an edge in artistic quality, though AI tools like Midjourney are closing the gap. My recommendation: use AI for main images and most alternative images, hire a photographer only for hero campaign shots that need a specific creative vision.

### Q: What's the best AI tool for Amazon images if I'm a complete beginner?

A: For beginners, I recommend Lovart. The ChatCanvas interface is conversational, which means you don't need to learn complex prompt engineering. The MCoT Engine handles many of the technical requirements (background purity, fill ratio, lighting) automatically. The Touch Edit feature makes corrections easy without regenerating entire images. Most beginners can produce their first Amazon-compliant main image within 30 minutes of signing up. Adobe Firefly is also beginner-friendly if you already have Photoshop experience, but the learning curve is steeper for pure generation work. Avoid Stable Diffusion and other open-source tools as a beginner — the setup and configuration required is significant.
