## Episode Summary

Three years ago, Dr. Amara Singh made a controversial decision: she removed the ban on AI tools from every course in the Parsons design curriculum. What followed was not the collapse of creative rigor that critics predicted, but a fundamental reimagining of what design education should be.

Dr. Singh has since published extensively on AI pedagogy, advised accreditation bodies on updated competency standards, and helped Lovart develop its Education tier — used by over 240 design programs worldwide. In this conversation, she argues that the question is no longer "Should we teach AI?" but "How do we teach design when AI is no longer optional?"

---

## Show Notes

### [00:02:00] The Decision to Embrace AI

**Lena**: Dr. Singh, take us back to 2024. What prompted you to remove the AI ban from Parsons' design courses?

**Amara**: Fear, honestly — but not fear of AI. Fear of what would happen to our graduates if we didn't prepare them. In 2024, I was already seeing job descriptions that required "AI-assisted design workflow experience." Meanwhile, our students were secretly using Midjourney and Lovart to complete assignments, then pretending they hadn't. We were creating a culture of shame around the very tools the industry was demanding.

So I asked my faculty a simple question: are we training designers for 2014 or for 2027? The answer was uncomfortable. Within a semester, we had rewritten all twelve core studio courses to integrate AI as a standard tool — alongside Figma, Adobe Suite, and the sketchbook.

### [00:06:45] What AI-Integrated Design Education Looks Like

**Lena**: Walk us through a typical assignment in an AI-era design classroom.

**Amara**: Let me give you a concrete example from Typography II, a second-year course. The traditional assignment was "design a type specimen poster for Garamond." Students would spend two weeks on research, sketches, and manual layout.

The redesigned assignment: "Design a type-driven brand identity for a fictional cultural institution. Use Lovart to generate 50 initial visual directions in 30 minutes. Curate your top 3. For each, manually refine the typography — kerning, hierarchy, optical adjustments — and write a 500-word rationale explaining why you chose the AI-generated direction you did and what human decisions you made to elevate it."

**Lena**: So AI becomes the divergent thinking tool, and humans handle the convergent thinking.

**Amara**: Precisely. What got cut from the curriculum was not creative decision-making — it was the rote production labor that consumed 70% of student time without teaching 70% of the skills. By removing that bottleneck, we freed up bandwidth for what actually matters: critique, refinement, conceptual thinking, and the ability to articulate design decisions.

### [00:13:20] The Competency Shift

**Lena**: What new competencies have you had to define for this AI-integrated curriculum?

**Amara**: Five core competencies now underpin every course:

1. **Prompt Literacy**: Not just writing prompts, but understanding how to iterate, constrain, and direct generative models toward specific outcomes. This is fundamentally a communication skill — it is about precision of language and clarity of intent.

2. **Curatorial Judgment**: When an AI generates 50 logo concepts in 10 seconds, the bottleneck shifts from production to selection. Students must develop the discernment to identify what works and, more importantly, articulate *why*.

3. **Ethical Reasoning**: Understanding training data, bias, copyright, and the societal implications of AI-generated content. This is now a standalone module in our first-year foundation course.

4. **Technical Integration**: The ability to move fluidly between AI tools and traditional design software — taking an AI-generated concept into Figma for layout refinement, or bringing a hand-drawn sketch into Lovart for contextual rendering.

5. **Design Authorship**: In a world where machines can generate visuals, what does it mean to be the author of a design? Students must define and defend their creative contribution in every project.

### [00:19:30] The Resistance and How It Evolved

**Lena**: Not every faculty member was on board. Tell us about the resistance.

**Amara**: Oh, it was fierce initially. Some faculty members felt we were devaluing craft — that spending 40 hours hand-kernning a poster was a rite of passage that built character and skill. They were not entirely wrong. There is value in craft labor. The question is: at what opportunity cost?

I had one professor — a brilliant typographer who had been teaching for 25 years — tell me, "If students don't suffer through the process, they won't respect the outcome." I asked him, "Do you still set type with metal blocks? Because that's what your predecessors said when digital typesetting arrived." He paused for a long time. Then he asked for a Lovart demo.

He now teaches our most popular elective: Human-AI Collaborative Typography.

### [00:24:10] Student Outcomes

**Lena**: What has happened to your graduates since this shift?

**Amara**: Our 2026 graduating class — the first cohort fully trained under the AI-integrated curriculum — reported a 94% job placement rate within three months. The industry average for design graduates is around 67%. Employers are telling us, point blank, that AI literacy is now a baseline expectation, not a differentiator.

But here is what is more interesting: our graduates are being hired into roles that did not exist three years ago. "AI Creative Director." "Visual System Designer." "Generative Brand Strategist." These titles reflect a fundamental shift — design leadership now includes managing machines as collaborators, not just managing other designers.

### [00:29:00] The Lovart Education Partnership

**Lena**: Parsons was one of the first institutions to adopt Lovart's Education tier. How has that shaped your program?

**Amara**: The partnership has been transformative for access and equity. Before Lovart, a student who couldn't afford a $2,000 MacBook Pro was at a disadvantage. Now, Lovart runs on any device with a browser — our students are generating professional-grade work from Chromebooks and library computers.

The Education tier also gives us curriculum templates, assignment frameworks, and assessment rubrics that we have adapted for our context. But the real value is that our students are learning the professional version of the tool they will use after graduation. No watered-down "education edition" — the same Lovart platform used by Nike, Airbnb, and thousands of small businesses.

### [00:34:45] The Cheating Question

**Lena**: Let's address the elephant in every AI education conversation: cheating. How do you assess authentic student work when AI can generate entire projects?

**Amara**: This is the wrong framing. In a design education, the product — the final image or layout — was never the primary evidence of learning. The process was. My best students have always been the ones who could explain why they made every decision they made.

Our assessment has shifted from "show me what you made" to "show me how you made it and why." Every project requires a process document: prompt logs, iteration screenshots, decision trees, and a critical reflection. If a student cannot defend their curatorial choices, they fail — regardless of how polished the final output looks.

We also use oral critiques more heavily now. A 15-minute conversation reveals more about design thinking than a portfolio PDF ever could. AI cannot fake design reasoning — at least not yet.

### [00:40:00] The Global Perspective

**Lena**: You have consulted with design schools in Nairobi, Mumbai, and São Paulo. How does the AI education conversation differ across contexts?

**Amara**: In the Global North, the conversation is dominated by anxiety — will AI replace designers, dilute creativity, de-skill the profession? In the Global South, the conversation is about access. AI tools collapse the resource gap. A designer in Lagos with a smartphone and Lovart can compete for global clients in ways that were unimaginable ten years ago.

I was in Nairobi recently, visiting a design incubator. One of the founders told me, "AI didn't take my job. It gave me jobs I couldn't reach before." That perspective — seeing AI as an access multiplier rather than a threat — is something Western design education desperately needs to absorb.

### [00:46:00] Advice for Design Students and Educators

**Lena**: Final question: what is your advice for a design student entering the field in 2027? And for an educator trying to update their curriculum?

**Amara**: For students: your value is not in your ability to execute — machines will always execute faster and cheaper. Your value is in your ability to see, to judge, to connect, and to care. Develop your taste mercilessly. Study art history, anthropology, psychology. The tools will keep changing; your eye and your judgment are your career.

For educators: start tomorrow. Do not wait for the perfect curriculum framework or the ideal AI policy. Pick one assignment in one course. Replace the labor-intensive production phase with an AI tool. See what happens. Your students are already using these tools. The question is whether you are going to help them use them well.

## Key Takeaways

1. **AI literacy is now a baseline job requirement**, not a differentiator — design education must reflect this.
2. **Shift assessment from output to process**: Prompt logs, decision rationales, and oral critiques reveal real learning.
3. **AI is an access multiplier**: It collapses the resource gap between elite institutions and under-resourced design communities.
4. **Five new competencies**: Prompt literacy, curatorial judgment, ethical reasoning, technical integration, and design authorship.
5. **Start small**: Replace one assignment's production phase; iterate from there.
