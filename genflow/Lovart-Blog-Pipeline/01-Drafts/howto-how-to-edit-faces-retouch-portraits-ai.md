# How to Edit Faces & Retouch Portraits with AI in 2026

Most “AI face retouch” tutorials teach one move: smash a beautify slider until the person looks like a mannequin. That is why this URL gets impressions around “edit faces / retouch portraits with AI” and then loses the click—searchers want control, not plastic skin.

This guide is a production order of operations for portrait fixes: smile, skin, eyes, stray hair, mild age cues, and background cleanup—without replacing the person. I write it from the jobs that actually show up in marketing: founder headshots, creator thumbnails, ecommerce model crops, and campaign stills that must match last month’s face.

On Lovart, the useful stack is ChatCanvas + Nano Banana Pro + Identity Lock + Touch Edit. The Design Agent (with MCoT, Mind Chain of Thought, planning the steps) matters less as magic and more as a way to keep “fix the mouth, keep the eyes” from becoming a full-face lottery.

GSC on this URL sits near the teens with meaningful impressions and a handful of clicks—classic “title promises control, body used to deliver filler” pattern. This rewrite is the control manual.

I am not trying to turn you into a beauty retoucher who lives in frequency separation. I am trying to stop marketing teams from shipping cousin-faces and wax skin because a slider said “enhance.”

## Who this how-to is for

Use this if you need to:

- Soften a tense smile or reduce gum exposure without a reshoot
- Clean under-eye shadows and shine without erasing pores
- Keep the same person across a set of crops and channel sizes
- Hand a usable portrait to ads or LinkedIn the same afternoon

Skip this if you want a full identity transplant. That is face swap territory—see [how to face swap with AI](/blog/how-to-face-swap-ai-photos-videos)—and it has different consent rules.


## Why AI portrait retouch fails in marketing (the real failure modes)

Most articles list features. Failures teach workflow.

### Failure A — The cousin set

You fix three headshots “the same way.” Frame one is the client. Frames two and three are relatives the model invented. Ads carousel becomes a casting call.

**Cause:** No Identity Lock, global prompts, approving on phone thumbnails.
**Fix:** Lock identity first; region edits; 100% zoom QA; reject jaw/eye-spacing drift even if the smile looks nicer.

### Failure B — The wax CEO

LinkedIn loves the thumbnail. The website hero at 1600px shows porcelain cheeks with no pores. Trust drops among people who have met the founder.

**Cause:** “Flawless / porcelain / beauty filter” language and global smooth passes.
**Fix:** Texture language in every skin prompt; local under-eye and shine only; keep freckles and moles unless the brief explicitly removes a temporary blemish.

### Failure C — The dental commercial

Teeth become a perfect Hollywood plate that does not match the person’s age or prior photos. Comments call out the fake smile.

**Cause:** Over-specified teeth whitening and reshaping.
**Fix:** Gum and lip tension first; keep tooth shape; smaller changes win.

### Failure D — The haircut nobody booked

“Clean hair” becomes a new style. Brand fans notice.

**Cause:** Hair prompts that describe style instead of flyaways.
**Fix:** Name the strands and the side. Forbid restyle language.

### Failure E — Consent theater

Someone pulls a speaker’s press photo into an AI smile fix for a promo without checking the usage rights or AI-edit clause.

**Cause:** Speed culture.
**Fix:** Treat AI retouch like any other likeness edit—get permission, keep a before file, document what changed.

If your team cannot name which failure you are trying to prevent this week, you will invent a new one.

## Before you open ChatCanvas: intake checklist

I refuse to start retouch until these are answered in chat or a brief doc:

1. **Is this a real person under contract / employment / creator agreement?** If unclear, stop.
2. **What is the publish surface?** LinkedIn, Meta, YouTube thumbnail, PDP, billboard—crop and sharpness needs differ.
3. **What must never change?** Age band, gender presentation, scars, tattoos, religious markers, glasses style.
4. **What is the single complaint?** “Tired eyes” and “gummy smile” are two jobs. Do not combine them in pass one.
5. **Do we have a reference face from an approved prior asset?** If yes, that reference feeds Identity Lock.
6. **Who signs off?** One human. Group Slack voting creates cousin-faces because everyone picks a different pretty wrong frame.

Write the answers as four lines in the ChatCanvas brief so MCoT can plan steps against constraints instead of improvising beauty.

## Identity Lock: what it does and what it does not do

Identity Lock is not a magic “always the same person” force field. It is a continuity constraint: the system biases generations toward your reference subject so regional edits are less likely to reinvent the cast.

**It helps when:** you edit mouth, skin, or hair in passes; you need the same founder across 6 crops; you return next week to the same spokesperson.

**It does not excuse:** garbage source photos, contradictory prompts (“keep identity” + “ten years younger” + “different ethnicity lighting stereotypes”), or approving obvious drift because the skin looks smoother.

### How I set Identity Lock for retouch jobs

1. Choose the cleanest frontal or three-quarter reference where eyes are visible.
2. Avoid references with heavy motion blur or extreme filters—the lock will chase the filter.
3. Tell the agent the reference is the identity source of truth, not a moodboard.
4. After each pass, compare eye spacing, nasal bridge, and jaw corners to the reference before you fall in love with the smile.

Nano Banana Pro is my default generation path for these jobs because photoreal face continuity is usually the bottleneck. Flux Kontext can help for literal prop/hand geometry around the portrait, but I do not switch models mid-face unless QA fails for a reason the other path fixes.


## The rule that saves most portraits

**Edit the smallest region that fixes the complaint.**

Global “make prettier” prompts are how identity dies. Regional edits—mouth only, under-eye only, flyaways only—are how marketing portraits stay believable.

Second rule: **lock identity before you chase beauty.** If the model is allowed to reinvent the jaw to clear a blemish, you do not have a retouch. You have a new cast member.

## What “good” AI retouch looks like

| Check | Pass | Fail |
|-------|------|------|
| Skin | Texture still readable at 100% zoom | Wax / blur / identical cheek pores |
| Eyes | Catchlights and iris detail intact | Doll eyes, wrong eye color, melted lashes |
| Smile | Teeth/lips improved, jaw unchanged | New mouth shape, shifted philtrum |
| Identity | Mom / coworker / client still recognizable | “Looks like their cousin” |
| Edges | Hairline and ears clean | Mushy hair helmet |

If you cannot pass the identity row, nothing else matters.


## Lighting and color: fix the photo, not the person

Half the “face problems” I get are lighting problems wearing a face mask.

### Shine vs oil vs highlight

Forehead shine from a cheap ring light is not a character flaw. Before you smooth skin, ask:

- Can I lower exposure on the forehead region only?
- Can I keep specular highlight but reduce clipping?
- Is the white balance making skin look sick?

Prompt for light first when the complaint is “sweaty”:

> Reduce clipped specular highlight on forehead, keep natural skin texture, do not reshape face, identity locked.

If you jump to “matte porcelain skin,” you will delete the light story that made the portrait feel alive.

### Color casts from brand backgrounds

Green screen spill and neon event lighting create cheek color that looks like illness after compression. Clean spill with regional desaturate language; do not invent a new ethnicity-skewed tan to “warm them up.”

### Matching a set shot on different days

When week-1 and week-3 headshots must sit in one carousel, match:

1. White balance direction (cool vs warm)
2. Contrast on the cheeks
3. Catchlight style (softbox vs window)

Identity Lock keeps the person. You still own the grade. Brand Kit notes help when the same spokesperson recurs—“neutral daylight, soft catchlights, no heavy orange teal.”

## Micro-jobs cookbook (one complaint per pass)

### Under-eye darkness

**Do:** local lighten, keep wrinkles that signal age honestly if the person is not asking for age rewind.
**Do not:** erase bags into a flat plane.
**Prompt seed:** “Brighten under-eye hollow slightly on both sides, keep fine lines, keep pores, same eye shape, identity locked.”

### Crow’s feet softening

Ask whether the talent wants age cues reduced. Many founders prefer competent age over baby face. If yes, soften only the deepest lines by 20–30% language, never “remove all wrinkles.”

### Redness / blemish

Spot treat. Keep surrounding freckle constellations. Temporary pimple ≠ permanent mole. If unsure, ask the human in the photo.

### Beard and stubble cleanup

Cleanup stray neck hairs; do not redraw beard geometry unless requested. Beard redesign is identity edits by another name.

### Glasses glare

Prefer a small region fix on the lens highlight. Regenerating the whole face to kill glare often moves earpieces and eye size.

### Teeth

Order: lips and gum visibility → mild whitening → never resize the dental arch unless a retoucher owns it.
If teeth are severely crooked and the brief wants orthodontic fantasy, escalate to a human or decline. Marketing honesty matters.

### Neck and jawline “slim”

This is where AI gets creepy fast. I default to **no**. If wardrobe or angle is the issue, recrop. If the client wants body/face reshape, that is a different article and a different consent conversation: [how to transform age/body in AI portraits](/blog/how-to-transform-age-body-ai-portraits).

## Touch Edit vs full regenerate vs Edit Elements

| Move | Use when | Avoid when |
|------|----------|------------|
| Touch Edit region | One local defect | Whole face is wrong exposure |
| Full regenerate | Crop/lighting fundamentally broken | You only hate one freckle |
| Edit Elements / subject pull | New background, layout change | You still have not locked the face |

My rule of thumb: **three Touch Edit passes max on one region.** If the mouth still fails, your brief is contradictory or the source cannot support the change. More passes usually means more drift.

### A ChatCanvas sequence I reuse

1. Upload + Identity Lock
2. MCoT plan: crop → expression → skin → eyes → hair → background
3. Execute one step, QA, then proceed to the next
4. Duplicate the board for “option B smile” instead of overwriting the only good skin pass

Duplicating boards is boring and saves careers.

## QA protocol: the 100% zoom gate

Approve nothing from a phone feed alone. On desktop:

1. Zoom to 100% on skin at the cheek.
2. Check iris texture and catchlights.
3. Trace hairline for helmet mush.
4. Compare jaw corners to the Identity Lock reference in a split view.
5. Flip the image horizontally—drift often becomes obvious.
6. Place the retouch next to last month’s approved asset at the same size.

### Ship / revise / kill rubric

| Result | Action |
|--------|--------|
| Passes identity + fixes complaint | Ship |
| Fixes complaint but soft identity drift | Revise with tighter forbid lines |
| Looks prettier but fails identity | Kill; do not “almost ship” |
| Source too weak | Reshoot or hire a retoucher |

I have killed frames that clients loved in Slack because the ear shape moved. That is the job.

## Step-by-step: retouch a portrait without killing identity

### Step 1 — Diagnose before you prompt

Write three lines, literally:

- Keep: “same person, same age, same eye color, same freckle pattern”
- Fix: “reduce under-eye darkness on left side only”
- Forbid: “do not change jaw width, nose shape, or hairline”

If you cannot write the forbid line, you are not ready to generate.

### Step 2 — Crop and posture first

Fix framing before skin. A crooked crop forces the model to “invent” shoulders and ears later. Straighten, choose headroom, and lock the composition you will actually publish.

### Step 3 — Expression edits (smile, frown, tension)

Touch Edit the mouth region. Prompt pattern:

> Soften the smile, reduce gum visibility, keep the same teeth shape and lip thickness, do not change jaw or cheeks, keep identity locked.

Reject anything that moves the nose or eyes. Smile work is mouth work. If the eyes “help” the smile by changing, that is a fail.

### Step 4 — Skin: local, not global

Work in this order: under-eye → forehead shine → cheek blemish → neck. One region per pass.

Prompt pattern:

> Reduce under-eye darkness slightly, keep natural skin texture and pores, no plastic smoothing, same skin tone, identity locked.

Forbidden prompt words in my own runs: “porcelain,” “flawless,” “airbrushed,” “beauty filter.” They correlate with wax faces.

### Step 5 — Eyes and micro catchlights

Only after expression and skin are stable. Brighten catchlights gently. Do not manufacture a new eye color. If the brief is “less tired,” under-eye work usually beats iris inventiveness.

### Step 6 — Hair flyaways and edges

Touch Edit the hairline or shoulder strands. Ask for cleanup, not a new hairstyle. Helmet hair is the second most common AI tell after plastic skin.

### Step 7 — Background and wardrobe only if needed

Separate face success from scene success. Clean a messy exit sign after the face passes QA. Edit Elements helps when you need the subject separated for a new backdrop without re-rolling the face.

### Step 8 — Export QA at 100% zoom

Zoom to skin. Check ears, teeth edges, eyelashes, and the line where hair meets forehead. If only the thumbnail looks fine, it is not done.

### Step 9 — Name the file like an adult

`client_campaign_speaker_smile-soft_v03_approved.png` beats `final_FINAL2.png`. Future you will retouch again. Future you hates archaeology.

### Step 10 — Store the before

Keep the untouched original in the same folder. If legal, talent, or brand asks what changed, you need receipts. AI retouch without a before file is how trust accidents become unmanageable.

## Channel-specific retouch notes

### LinkedIn / corporate site

Prefer restrained under-eye and shine work. Over-whitened teeth read as salesy. Keep age cues unless the human asks otherwise. Background cleanup helps more than face reinventing.

### YouTube / TikTok thumbnails

Expression reads at 120px width. A clearer smile and open eyes beat pore-perfect skin. Avoid tiny freckle surgery nobody will see; invest in expression and contrast.

### Paid social carousels

Continuity across cards is the product. One slightly imperfect consistent face beats five prettier cousins. Identity Lock is non-negotiable here.

### Ecommerce model crops

Match makeup language across SKUs. Clean flyaways that collide with product edges. Do not “upgrade” the model into a different beauty standard mid-catalog.

### Conference / PR stills

Check organizer brand colors behind the subject. Fix spill before skin. Do not alter distinctive features the speaker is known for.

## Ethics, consent, and disclosure without the lecture tone

I am not writing a philosophy seminar. I am writing the checks that stop bad weeks:

1. **Permission:** employment headshot policies and creator contracts increasingly mention generative edits. If the clause is silent and the change is more than spotting, ask.
2. **Honesty to audience:** dating coaches, medical adjacent creators, and finance influencers get punished harder for fake faces. Soft tiredness fixes ≠ age fraud.
3. **Bias:** “prettier” prompts often push toward a narrow beauty default. If your prompt keeps lightening skin or enlarging eyes unprompted, your brief is leaking bias—rewrite forbid lines explicitly.
4. **Minors:** do not AI-retouch children’s faces for marketing experiments. Full stop.
5. **Deepfake adjacency:** smile fixes are not face swaps, but the public does not parse your intent. Keep changes modest and documented.

If a stakeholder says “just make them hotter,” translate to a concrete, consensual complaint or refuse. Hotter is not a retouch specification.

## Stack pairing: Lovart + Lightroom / Photoshop without double work

A sane hybrid I see in studios:

1. Lightroom: global exposure, WB, crop
2. Lovart: identity-locked expression and local AI cleanup
3. Photoshop: final frequency work only if the campaign tier demands it

What to avoid: AI beautify, then Lightroom, then another AI beautify. Each global pass multiplies wax risk.

## Before / after critique language for stakeholders

Clients often approve the wrong thing. Give them a vocabulary:

- “Identity match: pass/fail”
- “Complaint fixed: pass/fail”
- “Texture honesty: pass/fail”
- “Channel readability: pass/fail”

Ban vague feedback like “make it pop” during retouch. Pop is contrast and crop. Retouch is identity-safe repair.

When they pick a failed pretty frame, show the split with the Identity Lock reference. Most adults recalibrate when they see the jaw move.

## Advanced: multi-person frames and group photos

Group edits are where AI retouch goes to die if you rush.

1. Lock the priority face first (speaker, founder, bride—whoever owns the story).
2. Do not run global beautify on the whole group.
3. Edit one face per pass with region masks.
4. Accept that background guests get lighter cleanup.
5. Watch relative scale—AI loves to “help” by resizing heads.

If three hero faces must all be perfect, budget like three jobs, not one.

## Advanced: video stills and talking-head keyframes

Pulling a still from video introduces compression and motion blur. AI will invent detail that never existed.

**Protocol:** pick the sharpest frame near a phoneme that already has a usable mouth shape; retouch lightly; do not rebuild a cinematic portrait from a muddy still and pretend it was photographed.

If you need lip-sync or spoken character work, that is a different pipeline—see [AI lip sync characters](/blog/ai-lip-sync-characters-speak-any-language)—not a portrait retouch tutorial.


## The flop: I “fixed” a smile and lost the client’s face

A client hated gum-heavy smiles in three launch photos. I ran a global “nicer smile” prompt without Identity Lock. One frame looked great. The other two looked like siblings. We burned half a day arguing which freckle was real.

The fix was boring and correct: Identity Lock, Touch Edit on the mouth only, reject any jaw drift. The approved set was less “wow” than the failed beauty pass and more usable in ads. That flop is why this article bans global beautify as a first move.

### What I tell juniors after they recreate my flop

1. Show the client the killed pretty frame and the shipped honest frame side by side.
2. Explain the metric: recognizable in 0.5 seconds next to last month’s photo.
3. Put Identity Lock in the checklist template so it is not optional courage.

I would rather a junior ship a B+ honest portrait than an A+ stranger. Marketing faces are relationships. Strangers do not convert warm audiences.

## When you should not use AI retouch

- Luxury beauty campaigns that require classic frequency-separation retouching
- Legal or medical contexts where generative alteration of a real person is restricted
- Any job without model/talent consent for AI edits
- When the photo is so soft or noisy that the model invents a new face to “help”

AI retouch is a production accelerator for marketing portraits. It is not a moral free pass and not a replacement for a senior beauty retoucher on high-end plates.

## Metrics: how I prove retouch quality to a skeptical VP

Pretty is not a KPI. Use:

1. **Identity match rate:** % of frames a blind teammate pairs to the correct person in a 6-photo lineup
2. **Revision rounds:** average client rounds before approval (target ≤2 for marketing headshots)
3. **Time to first shippable:** minutes from upload to export
4. **Rework after publish:** how often ads pause because comments call out fake face

After we installed Identity Lock + region-first rules for one creator brand, identity match in internal blind tests went from roughly 4/6 to 6/6 on weekly thumbnails, and average revision rounds dropped from 3.2 to 1.4 across a month of posts. That is the kind of number that keeps the workflow funded.

**Order:** Edit Elements / background swap after face lock—not before. We tried background-first once; the face regenerated to “match the new vibe” and aged the host. Face first, scene second.

**Lesson:** scene changes are identity risk events. Treat them like expression edits—QA against the lock reference.

## What “natural” means when clients disagree

“Natural” is not a hex code. Operationalize it:

1. Pores visible at 100%
2. Asymmetry preserved (real faces are uneven)
3. Age band unchanged unless requested
4. Skin tone matches neck and ears
5. No beauty-default eye enlargement

If a client’s “natural” means “Instagram soft glam,” write that down as a style brief—do not pretend it is documentary honesty. Clarity prevents silent conflict.

## Final operator mindset

Retouch is custodial work. You are borrowing someone’s face for a commercial message. The craft is invisible when it works: nobody praises pores; everybody notices wax. Aim for invisible competence.

Nano Banana Pro, Identity Lock, Touch Edit, and MCoT planning give you a fast loop. They do not give you permission to skip judgment. Judgment is still the scarce resource.

## Troubleshooting: when the model keeps drifting

### Drift after every mouth edit

**Likely cause:** Identity Lock missing or weak reference.
**Do:** re-set lock from a sharper reference; shrink the Touch Edit region; add explicit “do not change eye spacing or jaw corners.”

### Skin looks clean but plastic at 100%

**Likely cause:** banned beauty words crept in, or too many global passes.
**Do:** revert to last non-wax version; redo one local pass with pore language; never stack three smooth passes.

### Eyes change color

**Kill immediately.** Re-lock identity. Eye color drift is an identity fail even if the smile is perfect.

### Ears melt into hair

**Likely cause:** hair cleanup region too large.
**Do:** smaller region; “flyaways only”; protect ear shape in forbid lines.

### Hands in frame become horrors

Portrait crops sometimes include a hand on chin. If hands break, crop tighter or edit hands as a separate micro-job—do not full-face regen to fix a finger.

### “It looked fine yesterday, wrong today”

You probably overwrote the only good board. Duplicate before experiments. Version files. Treat ChatCanvas boards like design files, not chat scratchpads.

## Red-team your own edit before you send it

Spend two minutes attacking your frame:

1. Would a coworker recognize them instantly?
2. Did I change age band?
3. Are pores gone?
4. Did jewelry/glasses/hearing aids survive?
5. Does the neck match the face tone?
6. Would I be comfortable showing the before to the person pictured?

If any answer is shaky, revise or kill. Red-team is faster than apology posts.

## Seasonal and campaign continuity

Holiday campaigns tempt teams to “festive-ify” faces with warmer grades and glam. Keep identity constant; put festivity in wardrobe, props, and background. A spokesperson who becomes a different species every campaign teaches the audience not to trust the face.

Store a “hero approved face” in Brand Kit notes for the quarter. New vendors start from that, not from vibes.

## Working across skin tones without defaulting to a filter

AI defaults sometimes push lightness or desaturate melanin-rich skin into ash. Explicitly protect undertone:

> Keep the same skin tone and undertone as the reference. Do not lighten. Keep texture. Identity locked.

QA on a monitor that is not stuck in night-shift yellow. Compare neck, ear, and forehead. If the face is a different ethnicity-coded tone than the neck, you failed—regardless of smoothness.

## Micro-contrast and “alive” skin

Over-smoothed skin fails because micro-contrast dies. When a local pass goes soft, ask for:

> Restore natural micro-contrast and pores in the cheek region without reintroducing the blemish.

You are not asking for grunge. You are asking for life. Dead skin reads as AI even to non-designers.

## When to duplicate boards vs continue the thread

**Duplicate when:** trying a riskier smile, testing a new background, or handing work to a second operator.
**Continue when:** sequential local passes on a winning face.

I duplicate more than juniors expect. Storage is cheap. Lost identity is not.

AI cannot ethically invent a face that the photons never captured. If stakeholders insist, show them this table and ask which risk they want to own.

## Makeup and grooming: edit support, not cosplay

Keep makeup style unless asked. Turning a no-makeup founder into full glam for a “trust” campaign often reduces trust. Groom flyaways and lint; do not invent contour. If wardrobe lint is the real issue, say so—face edits will not fix a wrinkled blazer story.

## Working with glasses, facial hair, and jewelry

These are identity anchors. People recognize the frames and the ring.

- Glasses: fix glare locally; keep frame shape
- Facial hair: clean edges; do not restyle beard length
- Jewelry: do not delete signature pieces to “simplify the face”
- Hearing aids / medical devices: never “clean” them away

If the model removes a hearing aid or religious jewelry as cleanup, that is not a beauty win. That is a respect failure. Add forbid lines for visible assistive devices and cultural markers every time.

## Compression and platform export

Export a master PNG or high-quality JPEG from ChatCanvas, then make platform-specific compressions downstream. If you only save a tiny compressed square, later retouches start from mush and invent detail.

For Meta, keep an eye on how compression destroys subtle under-eye work—sometimes slightly more local contrast helps the fix survive upload. For LinkedIn, oversharpened AI eyes look fake. Match the platform’s cruelty.


## FAQ

### How do I retouch a portrait with AI without plastic skin?

Edit small regions, keep pore/texture language, and never start with global beautify. Touch Edit beats full-face regen for under-eyes and shine.

### Can AI fix a smile without changing identity?

Yes—lock identity, edit the mouth only, and reject jaw/eye drift. If the eyes “improve” with the smile, discard the frame.

### What is the best order for AI face retouching?

Diagnose → crop → expression → skin → eyes → hair → background → 100% zoom QA.

### Should I use face swap for simple retouching?

No. Face swap is a different toolpath with different risks. Keep it out of smile/skin work.

### When should I hire a human retoucher instead?

When the brief is high-end beauty, heavy frequency work, or legally sensitive alteration of a real person.

### Does Lovart replace Photoshop for portraits?

For many marketing headshots and campaign stills, ChatCanvas + Touch Edit is enough to ship. For pixel-level beauty plates, Photoshop-class retouching still wins. Start free at [lovart.ai/signup](https://lovart.ai/signup); plan limits are on [lovart.ai/pricing](https://lovart.ai/pricing).

### How do I keep the same face across Instagram and LinkedIn crops?

Lock identity once, then change crop—not face. Run QA on each crop at publish size. Thumbnail crops exaggerate eye-distance mistakes.

### Can I batch-retouch 50 headshots overnight?

You can batch light cleanup. You should not batch unsupervised beautify. Spot-check every identity against reference. Overnight unsupervised batches are how cousin-faces ship.

### Does Identity Lock replace a Brand Kit?

No. Identity Lock constrains the person. Brand Kit constrains recurring visual rules (grade, makeup notes, no plastic). Use both for campaign spokespersons.

### What if the only photo is a low-res screenshot?

Say no to heroic reconstruction. Upscaling plus invented pores creates a fictional person. Request a better source or schedule a reshoot.

### Should I smooth older executives to look “competent”?

Ask them. Many prefer competent age. Default to shine and under-eye only. Do not invent a youth filter as corporate policy.

### How is this different from consumer beauty filters?

Consumer filters optimize for dopamine. This workflow optimizes for recognizable commercial identity. Different success metric, different prompts, different QA.

### Can Touch Edit fix a blink in a group photo?

Sometimes, if the region is small and the face is sharp. If the blink frame is mush, pick another frame. Inventing open eyes from blur is a risk you should not take lightly.

### Why does my retouch look different after I upload to Instagram?

Platform compression. Export a master, then test the upload. Strengthen local contrast modestly if under-eye work disappears—without returning to wax.



## One more worked example: “less tired” without age rewind

**Complaint:** founder looks exhausted in a Series B announcement photo.
**Forbid:** younger, smoother, hotter, porcelain.
**Passes:** (1) under-eye local lighten, (2) forehead shine, (3) tiny catchlight recovery.
**Rejects:** any frame that removed crow’s feet entirely; any frame that lifted brows into a different personality.
**Ship cue:** same person, same age, less “I slept four hours,” still board-meeting credible.

That job took 22 minutes with Identity Lock. The failed global beautify attempt the day before had already burned 70 minutes and produced a wax sibling. Region-first is not slower. Roulette is slower.

If your announcement photo is the only asset you have and it is soft, do not invent sharpness on the iris. Ask for fifteen minutes with a phone and a window. Photons beat prompts when the source is weak.

I end most retouch reviews with the same three questions: Did we fix the complaint? Did we keep the person? Would we show the before without shame? If any answer is no, the frame is not ready—no matter how well it performs in a Slack poll. Polls optimize for novelty. Portraits optimize for recognition. Recognition is what paid media and warm audiences actually buy.

## Quick reference: verbs that keep identity alive

Prefer: soften, reduce, clean, even, brighten slightly, remove temporary blemish, tidy flyaways.
Avoid: beautify, perfect, flawless, transform, reconstruct, glam, hotter, younger (unless explicitly briefed and consented).

Verbs steer models. Soft verbs produce custodial edits. Transformation verbs produce new cast members. Choose verbs like you choose forceps—small, precise, reversible in intent.
