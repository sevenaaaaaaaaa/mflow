# How Architect Mateo Reyes Cut Concept Design from 12 Days to 4 Hours — And Won 3 Projects His Firm Expected to Lose

Mateo Reyes stared at the competition brief for the Mendoza Cultural Centre and felt a familiar dread settle in. 47 pages of program requirements, site constraints, and community consultation feedback. The client — a provincial government with famously difficult approval committees — wanted a "landmark" that also "respected local vernacular." They wanted innovation and tradition, ambition and humility, all in the same building.

His firm, a 30-person practice in Buenos Aires, had been invited to submit alongside three larger, better-resourced competitors. The senior partners had given the project a 10% chance. "We submit so they remember our name for the next one," they'd said.

Mateo had 96 hours until the concept submission deadline. His normal concept design process — hand sketching, digital modeling, rendering, iterating — took 12 days for a project of this complexity.

He opened Lovart, described the project in a paragraph, and generated 47 concept visualizations in 4 hours. His firm won the competition.

---

## The Background

Mateo, 34, is a project architect at a mid-size firm specializing in cultural and institutional buildings — museums, libraries, community centres, university facilities. The work is design-intensive. Every project requires a unique formal language that responds to program, site, and cultural context. Precedent research doesn't help when the brief specifically asks for "something we haven't seen before."

His concept design pipeline was effective but slow:
1. **Research & precedent (2-3 days):** Study site photographs, analyze program, review similar projects for reference
2. **Hand sketching (2-3 days):** Generate 20-30 rough spatial concepts — massing studies, parti diagrams, elevation studies
3. **Digital modeling (3-4 days):** Translate the 5-6 best sketches into Rhino for volumetric testing
4. **Rendering (2-3 days):** Export to render engine, set up lighting and materials, produce presentation-quality images for client review

Total: 8-12 days per concept phase. Mateo typically managed 2-3 competitions or proposals per quarter. The firm lost more projects than it won — not because the designs were weak, but because the larger competitors could afford to generate 50+ concept options and present the best 15, while Mateo's firm could only fully develop 5-6.

---

## The Pain Points

### 1. The Option Gap

Architectural competitions are won through exploration, not refinement. The firm that generates 50 massing studies discovers a configuration the firm generating 10 massing studies never considered. The economics of manual concept generation meant Mateo's firm was structurally disadvantaged against larger competitors — not in design talent, but in the volume of ideas they could afford to explore.

### 2. The Rendering Bottleneck

Rendering a single architectural visualization at presentation quality took 2-6 hours of setup and processing time (lighting, materials, camera positioning, render computation). For a competition submission requiring 8-12 renderings, the rendering phase alone consumed 2-4 days. This was largely mechanical work — translating a clear design concept into realistic images — that added zero creative value.

### 3. The Iteration Cost

Client feedback on concept presentations typically required 2-3 revision rounds. Each round meant re-rendering 5-8 images — another 2-3 days of production time. This iteration cost created perverse incentives: architects avoided bold design moves because revising a bold move took as long as developing it. The safest design was the one that required the fewest revisions.

## The Turning Point

Mateo discovered Lovart through an architecture technology newsletter that mentioned AI image generation for concept visualization. The article was skeptical — "AI can't do architecture" — but included examples that looked surprisingly competent as conceptual studies.

He tested it with a finished project: a library his firm had completed in 2024. He described the design brief to Lovart's ChatCanvas — "regional library in temperate climate, woven timber facade, community gathering space, relationship to adjacent park" — and generated images.

The AI produced 12 concept visualizations. Two of them closely resembled the firm's actual built design. Three others explored directions the firm had considered but abandoned due to time constraints — and one of those abandoned directions looked, in AI visualization, better than the built project.

"I sat there looking at an AI-generated image of a library that my firm could have built but didn't — because we ran out of time to explore that direction. That was the moment. We weren't losing competitions because our designs were bad. We were losing because our process couldn't afford to discover our best ideas."

## The Solution: How Mateo Uses Lovart

### The 50-Option Concept Sprint

Mateo's new concept workflow for the Mendoza Cultural Centre:
1. **Site & program input (30 minutes):** Feed the project brief, site photographs, and key constraints into ChatCanvas as context
2. **Mass generation (90 minutes):** Generate 50+ concept visualizations by varying prompt parameters — site integration, formal language, material palette, relationship to context
3. **Curation (60 minutes):** Select the 10-12 strongest directions based on fit to brief, aesthetic quality, and constructability
4. **Refinement (90 minutes):** Use Touch Edit to refine the selected concepts — adjust proportions, test material variations, enhance specific details
5. **Presentation prep (30 minutes):** Export selected visualizations, arrange in presentation deck

Total time: 4 hours. Previous process: 12 days. Number of concepts explored: 50+ vs the previous 5-6.

### AI as a Design Partner, Not a Replacement

Critical to Mateo's approach: the AI generates visual ideas. It does not generate architecture. Mateo applies his professional judgment to every AI concept — testing spatial logic, structural feasibility, program fit, and construction viability. Roughly 40% of AI-generated concepts are architecturally unworkable (impossible spans, physically incoherent spaces, circulation that doesn't function). The remaining 60% contain at least one viable spatial idea worth developing.

"The AI is not designing the building. It's showing me visual possibilities I hadn't considered. I'm still the architect. I still make every design decision. But instead of starting from a blank page with a pencil, I'm starting from 50 possibilities and editing down to the best 5. That's a fundamentally different creative process — and a faster one."

### Competition Strategy Transformed

For the Mendoza Cultural Centre, Mateo submitted 15 concept visualizations — more than any competitor — showing the building from multiple angles, in different lighting conditions, at different scales. The selection committee specifically cited the "depth of design exploration" and "range of considered options" as factors in their decision.

Since adopting the AI workflow, Mateo's firm has won three competitions they were "expected to lose" — projects where larger, better-resourced firms were the favorites. The common thread in all three wins: the volume of design exploration communicated a seriousness of effort that clients interpreted as commitment.

## FAQ

### Can AI actually design architecture, or is it just generating pretty pictures?

AI generates visual concepts — compelling images that suggest architectural ideas. It does not design buildings in the professional sense. AI images often contain physically impossible geometries, nonsensical structural logic, and circulation that doesn't function. The architect's role is to evaluate AI-generated concepts for viability, extract the useful spatial ideas, and develop them into buildable designs. AI is a concept exploration tool, not an architectural designer.

### How do I get AI to generate architecturally coherent images?

Key prompt strategies: (1) Include architectural language — "massing," "facade treatment," "spatial sequence," "sectional relationship"; (2) Specify materials — "board-formed concrete," "louvered timber screen," "structural glass"; (3) Describe the site relationship — "embedded in hillside," "cantilevered over water," "opening to courtyard"; (4) Reference program — "cultural centre," "library reading room," "museum gallery sequence." Specific architectural terminology produces more coherent results than generic visual descriptions.

### Can I maintain my firm's design style across AI-generated concepts?

Yes — through prompt language and reference imagery. Include stylistic descriptors that match your firm's language: "raw material expression," "clear structural legibility," "warm minimalism." You can also upload reference images of your firm's previous work as style anchors — the AI will bias toward that visual language. Mateo maintains his firm's "tectonic clarity" aesthetic by always including material and structural descriptors in his prompts.

### Is AI concept generation faster than hand sketching?

For volume: yes, dramatically (50+ AI concepts in the time it takes to sketch 5-6). For specificity: hand sketching still provides more precise control over spatial configuration. Mateo uses both: AI for rapid volume exploration, hand sketching for refining the 2-3 most promising directions. The tools are complementary, not competitive.

### Can AI renderings be used in client presentations?

Yes — and clients often can't distinguish AI-generated concept renderings from traditional renderings. The key is curation: select AI outputs that show coherent, buildable-looking architecture (even if the underlying design isn't fully resolved). Avoid showing AI images with obvious artifacts (impossible geometries, distorted figures, Escher-like spatial contradictions). Mateo's competition-winning submissions used AI-generated concept images alongside traditional diagrams and plans — the combination presented as thorough design exploration.

### Will AI replace architectural renderers?

AI replaces the mechanical work of rendering — the labor of setting up materials, lighting, and cameras to translate a completed design into realistic images. It doesn't replace the creative work of composing an image that communicates a specific design intention. Architectural visualization professionals are adapting by focusing on the creative direction and curation of AI outputs rather than the technical production of individual renderings.

### Can AI handle specific site constraints and building codes?

No. AI image generation operates on visual patterns and cannot process numeric constraints (setback requirements, height limits, floor-area ratios). Site-specific constraints must be applied by the architect during the refinement phase. Mateo's workflow: AI generates concept visualizations → he tests them against site constraints manually → only viable concepts proceed to development. Roughly 40% of AI-generated concepts fail the site-constraint test.

## Operator Reframe: What This Case Study Is Really About

Mateo's firm was losing competitions to larger shops with bigger visualization budgets. The fix was not prettier lies. It was more honest options earlier — massing, material mood, public-space moves — under a constraint brief the committee already published.

Four hours is a sprint for concept branches, not a claim that architecture became a half-day profession.

Gold sentence: competitions reward the team that can show tradeoffs fast without abandoning the site truth.

## Boundary Again

Sketches and concept renders from Lovart are persuasion and exploration tools. Engineering, accessibility detailing, and sealed drawings remain elsewhere.

## Constraint Brief Before Sprint

1. Site photos + sun path notes
2. Program areas
3. Vernacular cues (materials, roof logics) — not pastiche bans list
4. Committee language: landmark vs humility tensions written as tests
5. Identity Lock for the firm's sketch language (line, wash, people scale)

## 4-Hour Sprint Shape

Hour 1: extract constraint tests; ban tourist pastiche.
Hour 2: massing families in ChatCanvas (3 families).
Hour 3: raise one family with material moods; Touch Edit awkward people/cars.
Hour 4: board package + narrative of tradeoffs (not a single hero lie).

Winning three "expected to lose" pursuits correlates with clearer tradeoff storytelling. Keep win logs; avoid destiny myths.

## Failure Modes

1. **Pastiche cosplay** — fake "local" decoration. Fix: material truth refs.
2. **Scale lies** — plaza that cannot fit. Fix: site overlay discipline.
3. **Committee bait** — one glossy image, no alternatives. Fix: always show 2 rejected families.
4. **Credit theft** — juniors present AI boards as personal hand drawings without disclosure if firm policy requires it. Fix: process honesty.
5. **Time myth** — counting only generation minutes, ignoring prep. Fix: log full sprint.
