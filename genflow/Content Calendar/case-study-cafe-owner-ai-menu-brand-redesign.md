# How Café Owner Leila Haddad Redesigned Her Menu and Brand in One Weekend — And Lines Formed Around the Block

[IMAGE 1 PLACEHOLDER — Persona Scenario]

Leila Haddad's café, Qahwa Bayrut, was everything a neighbourhood coffee shop should be: excellent Lebanese coffee, fresh-baked ma'amoul, warm hospitality, a space that felt like your grandmother's sitting room. It was also slowly going out of business.

The problem was visible everywhere. The menu was a laminated A4 sheet with 47 items in 10-point Times New Roman — a wall of text that customers squinted at for 45 seconds before ordering "the usual." The storefront sign was sun-faded. The social media presence was an Instagram account with 190 followers that hadn't posted in 8 months. The logo was clip art Leila's nephew had designed in 2019.

"In Beirut, coffee culture is competitive. There are five cafés within walking distance of mine — all with beautiful branding, Instagram-worthy interiors, menus that feel like art objects. Customers choose where to drink coffee based on how the café makes them feel. My café made them feel like they were in a government office."

Leila redesigned her menu and entire brand identity in one weekend. Six weeks later, there were lines around the block. She's now planning a second location.

## The Background

[IMAGE 2 PLACEHOLDER — Conceptual Diagram]

Qahwa Bayrut opened in 2017 in Hamra, a bustling Beirut neighbourhood with a mix of university students, professionals, and longtime residents. Leila, 45, had left a corporate banking career to open the café — a lifelong dream that she'd funded with savings and a small family loan.

The café's first three years were steady. The coffee was good. The location was central. The regulars were loyal. But by early 2026, the competitive landscape had shifted. Three new specialty-coffee shops had opened within 500 metres. Each had strong branding, beautiful menu design, curated social media, and an aesthetic that attracted the young, social-media-active customers who drive café culture in Beirut.

"Suddenly, my café looked like it belonged to a different era. The coffee was still excellent. The pastries were still homemade. But the experience — the visual experience of being in my café, looking at my menu, following my Instagram — was from 2017. And 2017 was nine years ago."

---

## The Pain Points

### 1. The Menu-as-Experience Failure

A café menu is not a list of items and prices. It's the primary tactile and visual experience between the customer and the business. A well-designed menu communicates quality, care, and aesthetic intention before the customer tastes anything. A poorly designed menu — dense text, no hierarchy, unappetizing presentation — communicates the opposite. Leila's menu was actively undermining the quality of her coffee before customers ordered.

### 2. The Instagram Economy

Café culture in 2026 is inseparable from Instagram. Customers photograph their coffee, their pastry, the café interior, the menu — and those photographs drive discovery. A café with a beautiful, photographable brand gets free marketing from every customer who posts. A café with no visual identity gets no posts — because there's nothing worth photographing. Leila was invisible on the platform where her customers discovered new cafés.

### 3. The Price-Perception Gap

Leila's coffee was underpriced relative to quality — not because she wanted to be cheap, but because her menu design suggested "budget café." Customers perceived lower value because the visual presentation signaled lower value. The same cup of Lebanese coffee served from a beautifully designed menu at a beautifully branded café commands a higher price — and customers are happy to pay it, because the experience feels premium. The gap between Leila's product quality and her brand quality was costing her revenue on every transaction.

## The Turning Point

Leila's daughter, a graphic design student, showed her mother Lovart — she'd been using it for university projects and thought it could help with the café. Leila was hesitant: "I'm 45 years old. I don't know how to use design software. My relationship with technology is WhatsApp and online banking."

Her daughter spent 45 minutes teaching her the basics: describe what you want, review the output, select the best version, refine. No design skills required. No software complexity. Just clear description and curatorial judgment.

Leila's first prompt: "Lebanese coffee shop menu design, warm and inviting, traditional elements with modern typography, rich coffee brown and cream color palette, Arabic geometric pattern accents, clear hierarchy, appetizing food descriptions, Beirut neighbourhood café feel."

The AI generated a menu design. Leila looked at it. "I almost cried. It looked like a menu from one of those cafés I'd been envying — the ones with lines and Instagram followers. And it took 45 seconds."

## The Solution: Leila's Weekend Transformation

### Day 1: Menu and Core Brand

**Morning — Menu Redesign (3 hours):** Leila redesigned the complete menu — breakfast, lunch, coffee, tea, pastries, seasonal specials. She simplified from 47 items to 34 (removing dishes that rarely sold), organized into clear categories with visual hierarchy, added descriptive language (no more "Cheese Manousheh — $3" — now "Akkawi Cheese Manousheh: stretchy white cheese on freshly fired dough, za'atar edge, served with olive oil and fresh mint — $4.50"), and designed the physical menu as a tri-fold card rather than a laminated sheet.

**Afternoon — Brand Identity (2 hours):** Generated the new logo (Arabic calligraphy-inspired wordmark with a coffee-bean motif), color palette (deep espresso #3C1A0E, warm cream #F5EFE6, copper accent #B87333, sage green #7D9B76), and typography system.

**Evening — Coffee Cup & Packaging Design (2 hours):** Designed branded coffee cups (hot and cold), pastry bags, takeaway boxes, napkin embossing, and a loyalty card. Every touchpoint a customer would interact with.

### Day 2: Physical Space and Digital Presence

**Morning — Signage & Interior (2 hours):** Designed the new storefront sign, window graphics, interior wall menu, table-tent specials, and restroom signage.

**Afternoon — Social Media (2 hours):** Generated Instagram profile identity (profile photo, Story highlight covers, post templates in three formats), Facebook cover photo, Google Business profile imagery, and a "grand reopening" announcement suite.

**Evening — Grand Reopening Campaign (1 hour):** Generated invitation graphics, event signage, a special-event menu, and social-media countdown posts.

Total time: 12 hours across 2 days. Total assets created: 38 branded touchpoints. Total cost: $19 (Lovart Starter plan).

## FAQ

### Can AI redesign a café menu that actually improves sales — not just looks nice?

Yes — when menu psychology principles are incorporated into the design. Leila's menu redesign included: (1) simplified item count (47 → 34, reducing choice paralysis); (2) visual hierarchy (bestsellers highlighted, categories clearly separated); (3) descriptive language (ingredients, preparation, sensory details — not just item names); (4) strategic pricing presentation (no currency symbols, prices at line-end not in columns); (5) high-margin items positioned in "golden triangle" zones where eyes land first. The AI executed the visual design; Leila applied the menu psychology — learned from a single blog post on café menu optimization.

### How do I photograph my food for a menu if I'm not a food photographer?

Leila's approach: take smartphone photos of every dish in natural window light (near a large window, midday, no direct sun), use Lovart's photo enhancement to improve lighting, sharpness, and color balance, and — most importantly — use AI-generated lifestyle/context images as supplementary visuals. The menu featured 6 signature item photos (AI-enhanced smartphone shots) and 4 lifestyle images (AI-generated scenes of people enjoying coffee in a Beirut café setting). The hybrid approach — real for accuracy, AI for atmosphere — produced a menu that looked professionally photographed.

### What's the most important design element for a café rebrand?

The menu — because it's the touchpoint every customer interacts with, multiple times per visit. Leila's rebrand started with the menu and built outward (logo, signage, packaging, social media). This is the reverse of how agencies typically work (logo first, then applications), but it's the correct priority for a café: the menu is the primary sales tool. Design it first, define your visual language through it, then apply that language to everything else.

### Can I do a complete café rebrand over a weekend — really?

Yes — if you have clear creative direction and are willing to make decisions quickly. Leila's 12-hour weekend produced 38 brand touchpoints because she knew what she wanted (warm, traditional, Lebanese-modern) and evaluated AI output decisively. The time-consuming part of rebranding isn't design production — it's creative indecision. The AI eliminates production time; the business owner must eliminate decision paralysis. Know what you want before you start generating.

### How do I maintain my new brand after the initial redesign?

Use Lovart's Brand Kit as your brand guardian. Once the brand parameters are configured — logo, colors, fonts, visual style — every future asset (seasonal menu updates, social media posts, promotional materials, new signage) will automatically maintain brand consistency. Leila's staff now generate the daily specials board using the Brand Kit; the output always matches the café's visual identity regardless of who generates it. The brand maintains itself because the system enforces consistency.

### Will customers notice or care that the rebrand was AI-designed?

Leila's experience: zero customers have asked about production method. They ask about the coffee, the pastries, the atmosphere. They photograph the menu because it's beautiful, not because they're curious about its origins. The brand redesign attracted customers and increased revenue — those are the metrics that matter. Production method is an operational detail that customers experience indirectly (through the quality of the result), not directly (through knowledge of the process).

### How much does a café rebrand cost with AI vs traditional methods?

Leila's cost comparison: AI rebrand (Lovart Starter, $19) + physical production (sign painting, menu printing, cup printing — ~$800) = ~$819 total. Agency rebrand quote: $3,500-7,000 for design only, plus physical production costs. Freelancer rebrand estimate: $1,200-2,500 for design only. The AI approach doesn't eliminate physical production costs (you still need to print menus and paint signs), but it eliminates design production costs — which are typically 60-80% of a rebrand budget.
