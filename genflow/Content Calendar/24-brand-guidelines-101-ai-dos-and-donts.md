# Brand Guidelines 101 — Asking AI to Write Your Visual Dos and Don'ts

[IMAGE 1 PLACEHOLDER — Persona Scenario]

You know the feeling. You send a junior designer — or a freelancer, or a social media manager, or an intern — your brand assets. Logo PNGs. A color palette screenshot. Maybe a font file, if you remembered. Six weeks later, your Instagram feed looks like four different companies took turns posting.

The culprit isn't talent. It's ambiguity. You handed someone visual ingredients but no recipe. They're making decisions based on what "feels right" to them, which produces a different result than what "feels right" to you. Without explicit rules — dos and don'ts that survive the transition from your head to their screen — brand consistency is a lottery.

Brand guidelines solve this. And here's the part most people miss: you don't need to write them yourself. You can direct an AI to write them in 20 minutes, producing a document that's more structured and actionable than anything you'd draft at 10 PM on a Thursday.

## Why Visual Rules Matter More Than Visual Assets

[IMAGE 2 PLACEHOLDER — Conceptual Diagram]

Files decay. Your logo PNG gets resized, re-exported, placed on the wrong background. Your color hex codes get approximated. Your fonts get substituted when the recipient doesn't have the license. What doesn't decay is a rule — "never place the logo on a background darker than 60% gray" or "always use 24px of padding around the logo mark" survives every system migration, every team member change, every platform transition.

Brand guidelines aren't design police. They're decision-elimination machines. A good set of guidelines removes approximately 80% of the micro-decisions involved in executing a brand touchpoint. Should the logo be centered or left-aligned? Answered. Which font weight for subheads? Answered. What's the minimum photo size for a hero image? Answered. Each answered question is a decision the designer doesn't have to make, which means they can't make it wrong.

The paradox: constraints produce creative freedom. When a designer knows the boundaries, they can operate confidently within them. When they don't know the boundaries, they play it safe — and playing it safe across a team produces the visual mush that makes brands feel generic.

## How to Ask AI to Write Your Guidelines

The prompt structure is critical. You're not asking the AI to design your brand. You're asking it to document the rules that define your existing (or intended) brand. The distinction matters: the AI is a technical writer translating your vision into actionable documentation, not a creative director inventing a brand from scratch.

### Step 1: Define the Brand's Personality

Start with adjectives. Not aspirational adjectives ("we want to be seen as innovative") but operational adjectives that translate into visual decisions ("our palette is muted," "our spacing is generous," "our photography is documentary-style not studio-lit").

Prompt: *"Write brand guidelines for [Brand Name]. Brand personality: [5 adjectives]. Industry: [industry]. Primary audience: [describe]. Visual references: [2-3 brands whose visual language we admire, and why]."*

Example: *"Write brand guidelines for Verity skincare. Brand personality: clinical, transparent, warm, understated, intelligent. Industry: DTC skincare. Primary audience: ingredient-conscious women 25-40 who read labels and distrust marketing claims. Visual references: The Ordinary (clinical minimalism), Glossier (approachable warmth), Aesop (understated elegance — but we want less luxury, more lab)."*

### Step 2: Generate the Core Sections

The AI will produce a document covering the standard sections. Your job is to review and refine, not to accept wholesale. Here's what to look for in each section:

**Logo Usage.** The AI should specify: minimum size (in pixels), minimum clear space (the "padding" around the logo), acceptable color variations (full color, white, black), unacceptable uses (stretched, rotated, color-shifted, placed on busy backgrounds). Good guidelines include a visual "don't" section — crossed-out examples of what NOT to do. If the AI doesn't generate these, prompt: *"Add a 'Don't' section showing common logo misuse examples."*

**Color Palette.** The AI should provide: primary palette (3-5 colors with hex codes, RGB values, CMYK values if print is relevant), secondary palette (supporting colors for backgrounds, accents), and usage rules (which colors are text-safe, which are background-only). The most common omission: the AI may not specify accessibility contrast ratios. Add: *"Include WCAG AA contrast guidelines for each color combination."*

**Typography.** The AI should define: heading font and weights, body font and weights, accent font (if used), and a typographic scale (font sizes for H1 through body text). Crucially: "do not use all caps for body text" and "do not stretch or condense fonts" should be explicit rules if they apply.

**Photography & Imagery.** The most under-specified section in most guidelines. The AI should define: image style (documentary, studio, lifestyle, abstract), lighting quality (natural, studio, dramatic), color treatment (saturation level, warmth, filtering rules), subject guidelines (people? no people? product-only?), and composition preferences. Prompt separately: *"Write detailed photography guidelines for our brand, including lighting style, color treatment, subject rules, and five examples of acceptable and unacceptable image styles."*

**Voice & Tone.** If your guidelines include copy, the AI should define: brand voice characteristics (formal/casual, technical/accessible, warm/direct), sentence length preference, vocabulary rules (words you use, words you never use), and platform-specific tone considerations. This is often the weakest AI-generated section because voice is deeply specific. Treat it as a first draft and refine heavily.

### Step 3: Generate Specific Application Examples

Abstract rules are hard to follow. Concrete examples aren't. Prompt: *"Now apply these guidelines to three specific formats: an Instagram post, an email header, and a presentation slide. Show me what a correct execution looks like and explain which guidelines it follows."*

This produces the most useful part of the document — the "here's what good looks like" section. When a new team member asks "is this on-brand?", they compare their work to these examples, not to abstract color palette descriptions.

### Step 4: Add the "Get Started" Section

The most actionable guidelines include a quick-start checklist. Prompt: *"Add a one-page quick-start checklist — the 10 most important rules, in priority order, that someone needs to follow when creating their first brand asset."*

This acknowledges reality: most people won't read a 15-page guidelines document. They'll read a one-page checklist. Make the checklist the trojan horse for the full document.

## What the Guidelines Document Actually Looks Like

Here's the structure a well-prompted AI will produce:

1. **Brand Overview** — Who we are, what we believe, who we serve (1 paragraph)
2. **Logo Guidelines** — Logo variations, minimum sizes, clear space, color modes, misuse examples
3. **Color System** — Primary palette (with hex/RGB/CMYK), secondary palette, contrast rules, color proportions (60-30-10 rule)
4. **Typography** — Primary typeface, secondary typeface, type scale, usage rules, web fallbacks
5. **Photography & Imagery** — Style direction, lighting rules, composition guidelines, do/do not examples
6. **Graphic Elements** — Patterns, icon style, illustration style, decorative elements (if applicable)
7. **Application Examples** — Instagram post, email header, presentation slide, business card
8. **Voice & Tone** — Brand voice characteristics, vocabulary rules, platform-specific guidance
9. **Quick-Start Checklist** — One-page summary of the 10 most critical rules
10. **Asset Library** — Links to downloadable logo files, fonts, templates

Total document length: 8-15 pages. Time to generate with AI: 20 minutes. Time to review and personalize: 60-90 minutes. The AI provides the structure and 70% of the content. You provide the taste, the corrections, and the brand-specific decisions that only you can make.

## FAQ

### Should I use the AI-generated guidelines as-is?

No. Treat the AI output as a structured first draft. The AI excels at format, completeness, and consistency — it won't forget the hex code section or skip the clear space rules. It's less reliable on brand-specific nuance, voice authenticity, and strategic alignment. Review every section. Edit the personality description until it actually sounds like your brand. The value is the 80% of structural work the AI handled so you can focus on the 20% that requires human judgment.

### How do I make sure the AI's guidelines match my actual brand?

Provide specific visual references in the prompt. "Our brand feels like [Brand X] but with [key difference]." Upload your existing logo and color palette as reference images. If you have existing brand materials — a website, a pitch deck, social media posts — describe them: "Our current visual identity uses [describe palette] with [describe typography] and [describe photo style]." The more concrete input, the more accurate the output.

### Can I generate guidelines for a brand that doesn't exist yet?

Yes. This is actually one of the strongest use cases. Before you build the brand, define the rules. Prompt: *"Generate comprehensive brand guidelines for a hypothetical [industry] brand with these characteristics: [description]."* The guidelines become your design brief. Give them to anyone creating assets for the brand — including the AI — and you get consistency from day one instead of consistency retrofitted after six months of visual chaos.

### How often should brand guidelines be updated?

Review annually. Update when your brand strategy changes (new positioning, new audience, new product line) or when you encounter a recurring question that the guidelines don't answer (a new platform format, a new content type, a new team member role). Guidelines are living documents. The version you generate today should be v1.0, with a clear owner and a schedule for updates.

### Can Brand Kit really replace a guidelines document?

Not completely — they serve different functions. The Brand Kit enforces rules within Lovart's ecosystem. The guidelines document communicates rules to anyone working outside Lovart (freelancers using other tools, agencies, printers, developers implementing your brand in code). The ideal setup: guidelines document as the source of truth, Brand Kit as the automated enforcement mechanism. Both. Not either-or.

### Should I include a section on "what NOT to do"?

Yes. The "don't" section is often more valuable than the "do" section because it prevents the most common, most damaging mistakes. Logo stretched to fit a container. Logo placed on a photo without sufficient contrast. Wrong font used because the right one wasn't installed. Colors approximated from memory. A visual "don't" section with crossed-out examples teaches faster than any number of positive rules.

### Can I use this for client work?

This is one of the highest-leverage uses. Generate brand guidelines as part of your client deliverable. It positions you as a strategic partner (not just a pixel-pusher), it reduces the revision volume because the client has clear rules to reference, and it makes future work with the same client dramatically faster because you're operating within documented parameters. The AI-generated first draft takes 20 minutes. Handing a 12-page guidelines document to a client generates more perceived value than any single design asset.
