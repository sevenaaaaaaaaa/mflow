## Episode Summary

Brand managers occupy a strange seat in the modern organization. They are responsible for the most valuable intangible asset a company owns — its brand — yet they rarely have direct control over the people producing brand materials. Marketing creates campaigns. Sales builds decks. Social media ships posts. Partnerships produce co-branded assets. And the brand manager's job is to make sure it all looks, sounds, and feels like the same company.

David Okonkwo has been doing this for fifteen years, the last four at Patagonia, where he oversees global brand identity across 30+ countries and every channel imaginable — retail, e-commerce, activism, film, packaging, and internal culture. He has seen brand management evolve from PDF style guides to live design systems, from annual brand audits to continuous monitoring, and now — reluctantly and then enthusiastically — into the AI era.

In Episode 6, David joins host Jordan Park to discuss: what brand management actually involves at a global scale, where AI design tools fit (and where they do not), the governance challenge that keeps him up at night, and his advice for Lovart on building for brand teams.

## Key Discussion Points

### The Real Job of a Brand Manager

**Jordan Park:** Most people think a brand manager picks colors and approves logos. What is the actual work?

**David Okonkwo:** The actual work is maintaining coherence across an organization that is structurally designed to fragment. Patagonia has product designers, marketing designers, web designers, retail visual merchandisers, film editors, social media managers, partnership managers, and activist campaign leads — all producing visual material that carries the Patagonia name. My team of six people is responsible for ensuring that when a customer sees a Patagonia Instagram post in Tokyo, a store window in Berlin, an email in São Paulo, and a product tag in a REI in Boulder, they all feel like the same brand.

That is not a design job. That is a systems job. It is about building the infrastructure — the component libraries, the template systems, the approval workflows, the training programs — that makes brand consistency the path of least resistance rather than a bottleneck.

### Where AI Design Tools Help Brand Teams

**Jordan:** You have been experimenting with AI design tools, including Lovart. Where do they actually help?

**David:** In the long tail of brand production. Here is the shape of our design output: the top 20% of assets — the homepage, the seasonal campaign, the product launch hero imagery — are produced by our in-house creative team with full craft attention. They are art-directed, iterated, and reviewed over weeks. AI does not touch them and probably should not.

But the bottom 80% — the localized social media graphics for 30 markets, the reseller ad creatives, the internal event signage, the trade show booth graphics, the recruitment posters, the partnership co-branded assets — that is where the volume is. And that is where the quality variance happens because local teams are adapting templates by hand or, worse, building things from scratch.

AI design tools solve a real problem here: they let us create a locked-down brand template — "here is the Patagonia social post format with correct fonts, colors, logo placement, and tone" — and let local teams generate variations by describing what they need in natural language, within the guardrails. The local team in Japan does not need to understand our type system. They need to type "a post promoting our winter jacket line with Japanese copy and a Hokkaido winter landscape background," and get something that looks unmistakably like Patagonia.

### The Governance Challenge

**Jordan:** "Within the guardrails" is doing a lot of work in that sentence. How do you enforce that?

**David:** This is the heart of the problem. Current AI design tools are too permissive. If I lock the color palette, the AI might still produce a composition that feels off-brand — the wrong photographic style, the wrong whitespace ratio, the wrong energy. Brand is not just colors and fonts. It is a sensibility. And sensibilities are hard to encode in rules.

What I want — and what I have told the Lovart team directly — is a brand governance layer that goes beyond "use these hex codes." I want to define: approved photographic styles (documentary, not glossy), approved illustration styles (line art, not 3D render), approved whitespace ratios (generous, not crowded), approved typographic treatments (headline over image, not text-heavy), and tonal guardrails (earnest, not ironic; hopeful, not urgent).

If an AI design tool can enforce those softer constraints — not just the hard ones — it becomes a brand management platform, not just a design tool. And a brand management platform is worth 10× what a design tool costs.

### The Tension Between Speed and Control

**Jordan:** There is an inherent tension, though. More control means less speed. The whole promise of AI design is speed.

**David:** Correct. And that tension is productive, not destructive. The right question is not "speed or control?" It is "speed for whom, and control for whom?"

The local marketing manager in a regional office needs speed. They have 45 minutes to produce a social post that goes out today. They should not be making brand decisions — they should be operating within a sandbox where brand decisions have already been made for them.

The global brand team — my team — needs control. We should be able to define the sandbox: its walls, its floor, what tools are inside, what the acceptable outputs look like. And we should be able to update the sandbox centrally and have it propagate everywhere instantly.

That separation of concerns — brand team defines the system, local teams operate within it — is the organizational design that AI design tools should optimize for. Not "everyone is a designer now." That slogan is a governance nightmare.

### The Template Trap

**Jordan:** You mentioned templates earlier. Some people argue templates are the enemy of creativity.

**David:** Templates are the enemy of creativity when they are the only option. When the central team ships 50 locked templates and says, "Use these, do not deviate," you get visual monotony and local teams who feel disrespected.

The better model — and what I am pushing for with our AI design tooling — is **parameterized brand systems.** Instead of 50 fixed templates, you define a brand system with parameters: color palette, typography scale, spacing rhythm, approved illustration style, approved photo treatment, logo placement rules. Then local teams get an AI canvas that generates within those parameters. Every output is brand-compliant, but no two outputs are identical.

That is the vision. We are not there yet. But the components are emerging.

### How Brand Teams Should Evaluate AI Design Tools

**Jordan:** Practical question for the brand managers listening. What should they look for when evaluating an AI design tool?

**David:** Five criteria:

1. **Brand Kit depth:** Does it let you define more than colors and a logo? Can you set typography rules, spacing systems, approved asset libraries, and compositional preferences?

2. **Governance granularity:** Can you lock some elements and leave others flexible? A locked color palette with flexible layout is very different from a fully locked template.

3. **Permission architecture:** Can you separate who defines brand rules from who operates within them? If everyone is an admin, you have no governance.

4. **Version propagation:** If you update the brand kit, do all previously generated designs break? Do they update? Do you get a choice? This matters enormously at scale.

5. **Audit capability:** Can you see what has been generated, by whom, and whether it violates brand standards? Without audit, you are governing blind.

On all five, the industry is at maybe 40% maturity. The tools that get to 80% in the next 18 months will capture the brand management market.

### AI and the Future of Brand

**Jordan:** Big question to close. Does AI change what a brand is?

**David:** It changes how brand is produced, not what brand is. Brand is still the sum of every interaction someone has with your company, and the feeling those interactions leave. That has not changed since the first blacksmith hung a sign above his door.

What changes is the surface area. When AI lowers the cost of visual production to near zero, the volume of brand touchpoints explodes. Every customer service email can carry custom branded graphics. Every product recommendation can have a personalized visual treatment. Every internal presentation can look like it came from the marketing department.

That sounds like an efficiency win. But it is also a brand risk multiplied by a thousand. More touchpoints means more opportunities to get it wrong. The brand manager's job does not get easier in the AI era. It gets harder and more important. The tools just change.
